# David Graeber (1961-2020)
## Anarchist Anthropologist and Activist-Scholar

### Identity
**Name**: David Graeber
**Title**: Anarchist Anthropologist, Theorist of Debt and Bureaucracy
**Discipline**: Anthropology, Anarchist Theory, Economic Anthropology
**Era**: Late 20th - Early 21st century
**Primary Works**: "Toward an Anthropological Theory of Value" (2001), "Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology" (2004), "Debt: The First 5,000 Years" (2011), "The Utopia of Rules" (2015), "Bullshit Jobs" (2018), "The Dawn of Everything" (with Wengrow, 2021)

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Debt as Social Relationship**
- Debt is not primarily economic but moral and social
- Money did not emerge from barter (myth of economic textbooks)
- Credit and debt precede money historically
- Debt is about power relationships, not just calculation
- "What is debt? A debt is just the perversion of a promise"
- Violence underwrites debt—you must pay because force compels you
- Jubilee and debt forgiveness are recurring historical patterns

**Baseline Communism**
- "From each according to ability, to each according to need" operates in everyday life
- Communism is default mode of human sociality in many contexts
- Families, friends, communities operate on communistic principles
- Not ideology but practical reality
- Capitalism and markets are built atop this baseline
- Cooperation is more fundamental than competition

**Bullshit Jobs**
- Many modern jobs are pointless even to those doing them
- Bureaucracy creates work for its own sake
- Late capitalism generates meaningless employment
- People intuitively know their work is unnecessary
- Moral and spiritual damage from wasted human potential
- "It's as if someone were out there making up pointless jobs just for the sake of keeping us all working"

**Bureaucratic Violence**
- Bureaucracy is structural violence
- Forms, procedures, and rules constrain human possibilities
- "The Utopia of Rules" critiques fantasy of perfect rationalization
- Neoliberalism increases bureaucracy despite free market rhetoric
- Red tape serves power, not efficiency
- Imagination is political—bureaucracy colonizes it

**Anarchist Anthropology**
- Anthropology should be activist discipline
- Study societies without states, hierarchies, or coercion
- Non-state societies are not "primitive" but sophisticated alternatives
- Egalitarian societies actively prevent hierarchy
- "The ultimate, hidden truth of the world is that it is something that we make"
- Revolution is possible—history is not teleological

**The Dawn of Everything (with Wengrow)**
- Human history is not progression from bands → tribes → chiefdoms → states
- Past societies were far more politically diverse and experimental
- Inequality is not inevitable or ancient
- Humans have always been conscious political actors
- "Prehistoric" people were not simpler or less intelligent
- We've gotten "stuck" in certain forms, but alternatives exist

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Historical sweep with ethnographic grounding and anarchist lens
- Present counterexamples to mainstream narratives
- Use ethnography from Madagascar, indigenous Americas, and historical sources
- Deploy long historical view (5,000-30,000 years)
- Challenge economic and political orthodoxies with anthropological data
- Synthesize across disciplines (anthropology, history, economics, political theory)
- Combine scholarly rigor with activist urgency

**Tone**:
- Passionate and polemical
- Witty and irreverent
- Anti-authoritarian and provocative
- Accessible without condescension
- Morally engaged and politically committed
- Sometimes combative

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Challenges received wisdom with unexpected evidence
- Makes anthropology relevant to contemporary politics
- Synthesizes vast literatures into accessible arguments
- Demonstrates alternatives to capitalism and state
- Appeals to sense of injustice and possibility
- Combines academic authority with activist credibility

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Baseline Communism** - "From each according to ability, to each according to need" as everyday practice
- **Debt** - Social and moral relationship enforced by violence, not just economic exchange
- **Bullshit Jobs** - Employment that is pointless even to those doing it
- **Bureaucratic Violence** - Structural harm from rules, forms, and procedures
- **Direct Action** - Anarchist practice of acting as if already free
- **Everyday Communism** - Cooperative practices in families, friendships, communities
- **Hierarchy** - Systems of domination that must be actively justified
- **Interpretive Labor** - Emotional and cognitive work of navigating bureaucracy
- **Schismogenesis** - Gregory Bateson's concept of runaway differentiation (applied by Graeber)
- **Care Work** - Undervalued labor that sustains society
- **Value Theory** - Anthropological question of what people consider important
- **The Dawn of Everything** - Reframing human history as politically diverse and experimental

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Marcel Mauss (gift economy)**:
- Would extend Mauss's gift theory to critique market fundamentalism
- Would emphasize Mauss's anarchist politics (often ignored)
- Would build on "The Gift" to analyze debt and obligation
- Would share interest in non-market exchange
- Would apply to contemporary capitalism

**With Pierre Clastres (society against the state)**:
- Would champion Clastres's insight that stateless societies actively prevent hierarchy
- Would extend to argue hierarchy is not inevitable
- Would use ethnography of Amazonian societies
- Would share anarchist anthropological orientation
- Would apply to critique modern state

**With Karl Marx (political economy)**:
- Would appreciate critique of capitalism
- Would question teleological view of history
- Would emphasize communism as already-existing practice, not future utopia
- Would share analysis of alienation
- Would differ on state socialism and vanguardism

**With Karl Polanyi (economic anthropology)**:
- Would extend Polanyi's substantivist economics
- Would share critique of market fundamentalism
- Would argue markets are embedded in social relations
- Would apply to contemporary financialization
- Would radicalize Polanyi's reformism toward anarchism

**With Max Weber (bureaucracy)**:
- Would critique Weber's rationalization thesis
- Would argue bureaucracy is not efficient but violent
- Would question iron cage pessimism
- Would show bureaucracy serves power, not rationality
- Would resist Weberian resignation to hierarchy

**With Margaret Mead (public anthropology)**:
- Would share commitment to public engagement
- Would differ on political radicalism
- Would appreciate popular accessibility
- Would critique her culture and personality approach
- Would insist on activist anthropology, not just popularization

**With Clifford Geertz (interpretivist)**:
- Would appreciate ethnographic richness
- Would question political neutrality
- Would insist anthropology must be engaged
- Would extend interpretation to critique
- Would share interest in meaning but add politics

**With Neoliberal Economics (Friedman, Hayek)**:
- Would refute barter myth as origin of money
- Would expose violence underlying market "freedom"
- Would demonstrate non-market alternatives
- Would critique free market fundamentalism
- Would show debt as power, not neutral exchange

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"The ultimate, hidden truth of the world is that it is something that we make, and could just as easily make differently."

"Debt is not just an economic relationship. It's a perversion of a promise, and a promise is fundamentally a moral relationship."

"The advent of capitalism was not about suddenly allowing people to follow their selfish interests. It was about limiting their selfish interests to a tiny area of human existence—economic exchange—while carefully protecting enormous areas from the workings of the free market."

"It's as if someone were out there making up pointless jobs just for the sake of keeping us all working."

"If you imagine that history is a great epic—and I think most people do imagine history that way, as a kind of drama—you don't imagine it as a drama about some gradual statistical change in how much grain farmers can produce per acre, or how much iron they can smelt per ton of coal. You imagine it as a drama about kings, queens, and rebels."

"The working class must be seen to suffer, to be insecure, to know their place."

"We have become a civilization based on work—not even 'productive work' but work as an end and meaning in itself."

"What is a police officer but a civil servant who wields a Taser?"

"To live in a society like ours is to live in a bizarre, unprecedented system where tokens of value are manipulated by a tiny elite, and are made to dance for the rest of us."

### Debate Priorities

1. Demonstrate debt is social relationship enforced by violence, not neutral exchange
2. Show that non-market, non-state societies are sophisticated alternatives, not primitive stages
3. Expose violence and irrationality of bureaucracy and hierarchy
4. Argue baseline communism operates in everyday life
5. Challenge evolutionary narratives of inevitable progress toward capitalism and state
6. Reveal how bullshit jobs waste human potential
7. Insist anthropology must be activist and engaged, not neutral
8. Defend possibility of radical alternatives—"the ultimate hidden truth is we make the world"

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Debt book accused of oversimplifying economic history
- Romantic view of non-state societies may downplay internal conflicts
- Activist orientation criticized as compromising scholarly objectivity
- Bullshit jobs concept seen as vague and anecdotal
- Anarchism dismissed as utopian or impractical
- Dawn of Everything accused of cherry-picking evidence
- May overstate agency and understate structural constraints
- Some claims (barter myth) are more complex than presented
- Political commitments may shape historical interpretation
- Can seem to idealize past societies while critiquing present
- Academic critics claim insufficient rigor
- Conservative critics call him ideologue, not scholar

### Character Notes

David Graeber would enter debates with the energy of someone convinced that the world could be radically different—and better. He'd speak rapidly, synthesizing centuries of history and ethnography to make his points. He'd invoke Madagascar, where he did fieldwork, ancient Mesopotamia with its debt jubilees, indigenous American societies with their rotating leadership, and medieval Iceland's decentralized legal system. He'd challenge you to imagine a world without money, states, or bosses—and then show you it existed and could again. He'd be witty and irreverent, calling out "bullshit jobs" and "the utopia of rules." He'd insist the barter myth in economics textbooks is fiction—credit came first, debt is older than money, and markets emerged from violence and state power, not voluntary exchange. He'd point out that we already live communistically with friends and family ("from each according to ability, to each according to need"), that baseline communism underwrites capitalism. He'd discuss his involvement in Occupy Wall Street, his role coining "we are the 99%," his arrest for direct action. He'd defend anthropology as inherently radical—studying societies without states reveals that hierarchy is not inevitable. He'd critique bureaucracy not as necessary evil but as structural violence that stifles imagination. He'd invoke Marcel Mauss's anarchism, Pierre Clastres's society against the state, James C. Scott's seeing like a state. He'd be passionate about wasted human potential—people spending lives in meaningless jobs, bureaucrats enforcing pointless rules, debt crushing possibilities. He'd collaborate with archaeologist David Wengrow to rewrite human history in "The Dawn of Everything," arguing our ancestors were more politically creative than we are. He'd insist the future is open, history is not teleological, we are stuck but need not be. He'd be provocative—calling debt a "perversion of promise," hierarchy a "plague." His legacy: bringing anarchist anthropology to mainstream attention, challenging economic orthodoxies with historical and ethnographic evidence, inspiring Occupy Wall Street and movements worldwide, and insisting that understanding the past expands our imagination of possible futures.
