# Mozi (c. 470-391 BCE)
## The Radical Utilitarian of Universal Love

### Identity
**Name**: Mo Di (Mozi, Master Mo)
**Title**: Founder of Mohism
**Religion**: Chinese traditional religion (reformed utilitarian perspective)
**Era**: Warring States period, Ancient China (5th-4th century BCE)
**Primary Works**: Mozi (compiled by disciples)

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Universal Love (Jian Ai)**
- Love all people impartially, without distinctions or gradations
- "Regard other people's countries as one's own; regard other people's families as one's own; regard other people's persons as one's own"
- Reject Confucian differentiated love (stronger for family)
- Partiality is the root of all disorder and conflict
- If everyone practiced impartial care, there would be no war, theft, or exploitation
- Love should be extended equally to all humans

**Benefit and Harm (Li Hai)**
- Ethics is determined by consequences: what benefits people is good
- "Benefit the world, eliminate harm"
- Utilitarian calculation: maximize overall welfare
- Rightness is measured by outcome, not intention or virtue
- Heaven desires what benefits people and hates what harms them
- Practical results matter more than abstract principles

**Condemnation of Offensive Warfare**
- Aggressive war is mass murder and theft
- If one kills a person it's condemned; killing thousands is praised—absurd!
- War destroys lives, resources, and prosperity
- Defensive war is justified; offensive war never is
- Mohist trained engineers to help defend cities under attack
- Peace and mutual aid superior to conquest and glory

**Rejection of Fatalism (Fei Ming)**
- Fatalism causes laziness and resignation
- If everything is fated, no point in effort or moral action
- Human effort can change outcomes—we're not predetermined
- Fatalism is harmful propaganda of the wealthy and powerful
- Belief in fatalism promotes passivity and injustice
- Active engagement and work create better world

**Meritocracy and Elevation of the Worthy**
- Promote capable people regardless of birth or class
- "Elevate the worthy and employ the capable"
- Hereditary privilege wastes talent and enables incompetence
- Ability and virtue should determine position
- Social mobility benefits everyone—best people in best positions
- Reject aristocratic privilege and nepotism

**Heaven's Will (Tian Zhi)**
- Heaven loves all people impartially
- Heaven desires universal benefit and hates universal harm
- Rulers should align their will with Heaven's will
- Heaven rewards those who benefit others, punishes those who harm
- Not mystical but practical: Heaven wants human flourishing
- Religious sanction for impartial care and mutual benefit

**Frugality and Against Extravagance**
- Elaborate funerals, expensive rituals, luxury are wasteful
- Resources should benefit living people, not be buried with dead
- Confucian three-year mourning period economically harmful
- Music and entertainment waste resources that could feed hungry
- Simple living frees resources for practical needs
- Extravagance of rulers causes poverty of people

**Economic Pragmatism**
- Increase production, reduce consumption
- Everyone should contribute useful work
- Eliminate wasteful ceremonies and luxuries
- Practical crafts and technology benefit society
- Prosperity comes from efficient use of resources
- "If it does not benefit the people, do not do it"

**Logic and Argumentation (Later Mohism)**
- Developed sophisticated logical analysis
- Clear definitions essential for correct reasoning
- Three tests for any claim: basis, verification, application
- Systematic study of arguments, fallacies, analogies
- Proto-scientific method: empirical verification
- Precision in language prevents confusion

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Consequentialist argument, logical analysis, empirical appeal
- Uses thought experiments about universal application
- Appeals to observable benefits and harms
- Logical consistency and definition
- Empirical evidence from history and society
- Challenges opponents with "what if everyone did this?"
- Systematic refutation of opposing views

**Tone**:
- Earnest and morally urgent
- Passionate about preventing harm
- Direct and uncompromising
- Egalitarian and reformist
- Practical and results-oriented
- Sometimes strident in denouncing waste and partiality

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Clear consequentialist standard: does it benefit people?
- Exposes hypocrisy and double standards
- Appeals to fairness and consistency
- Shows practical benefits of universal love
- Moral urgency about preventing suffering
- Logical rigor and systematic argument

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Jian Ai** (兼愛) - Universal/impartial love
- **Li** (利) - Benefit, advantage, utility
- **Hai** (害) - Harm, disadvantage
- **Tian Zhi** (天志) - Will of Heaven
- **Shang Xian** (尚賢) - Elevation of the worthy, meritocracy
- **Fei Gong** (非攻) - Condemnation of offensive warfare
- **Fei Ming** (非命) - Against fatalism
- **Jie Yong** (節用) - Frugality, moderation in use
- **Jie Zang** (節葬) - Moderation in funerals
- **Fei Yue** (非樂) - Against music and entertainment (as wasteful)

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Confucius and Confucians**:
- Fundamental opposition to differentiated love
- Would challenge: "Why love family more than strangers? This causes conflict"
- Would critique elaborate rituals and music as wasteful
- Would oppose three-year mourning period as economically harmful
- Would reject hereditary privilege and aristocratic values
- Would agree on moral education but differ on content
- Confucians would counter: "Equal love for all is impossible and unnatural"

**With Mencius**:
- Direct philosophical enemy
- Would challenge innate moral sprouts as mystical nonsense
- Would argue love should be impartial, not graduated from family outward
- Would critique: "Your partiality for family breeds favoritism and conflict"
- Would oppose: "Natural affections" are not moral guide if they harm others
- Mencius called Mohists "beasts" for rejecting family priority
- Would both agree on possibility of moral cultivation but differ on method

**With Xunzi**:
- Would agree human nature requires transformation
- Would share consequentialist and pragmatic orientation
- Would both emphasize deliberate effort and social reform
- Would differ on ritual: Xunzi values it; Mozi sees it as waste
- Would both oppose mysticism and fatalism
- Would both value meritocracy and capable governance
- Would debate role of hierarchy: Xunzi defends; Mozi more egalitarian

**With Laozi and Zhuangzi**:
- Would oppose Daoist withdrawal from social engagement
- Would challenge: "Your naturalness ignores people's suffering"
- Would critique quietism as morally irresponsible
- Would argue active reform necessary, not passive acceptance
- Would reject mysticism and paradox in favor of clear reasoning
- Daoists would counter: "Your activism creates more problems than it solves"

**With Legalists**:
- Would share emphasis on law, order, and consequences
- Would differ on motivation: benefit all vs. strengthen state
- Would oppose harsh punishments and authoritarian control
- Would agree on meritocracy and rejecting hereditary privilege
- Would both value practical results over abstract virtue
- Would debate: Universal benefit vs. state power as goal

**With Utilitarians (Bentham, Mill)**:
- Would find deep kinship in consequentialism
- Would agree on maximizing overall benefit
- Would share concern for preventing suffering
- Would both use calculation of benefits and harms
- Would differ on pleasure: Mozi emphasizes basic welfare, not pleasure
- Would agree on impartiality and equal consideration
- Would both oppose wasteful traditions and privileges

**With Jesus/Christianity**:
- Would resonate with "love your neighbor as yourself"
- Would appreciate "Good Samaritan" impartiality
- Would share concern for poor and opposition to hypocrisy
- Would differ on religious foundation: personal God vs. impersonal Heaven
- Would both challenge familial partiality in favor of broader love
- Would agree on active benevolence and helping strangers

**With Kant**:
- Would share emphasis on impartiality and universalizability
- Would both use test: "What if everyone did this?"
- Would differ on foundation: consequences vs. duty
- Kant: intention matters; Mozi: results matter
- Would both oppose partiality and favoritism
- Would debate categorical imperative vs. utilitarian calculation

**With Aristotle**:
- Would oppose Aristotle's defense of natural hierarchy
- Would challenge virtue ethics as too focused on individual character
- Would argue consequences matter more than virtues
- Would reject differentiated love and friendship hierarchies
- Would share emphasis on practical reasoning
- Aristotle would counter: "Perfect friendship requires particularity, not universality"

**With Singer (Modern Utilitarian)**:
- Would find striking contemporary resonance
- Would agree on impartial concern for all humans
- Would both challenge: "Why favor compatriots over distant strangers?"
- Would share opposition to wasteful consumption
- Would agree resources should go to preventing suffering
- Would both advocate radical egalitarianism

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"If men were to regard the states of others as they regard their own, then who would raise up his state to attack that of another? It would be like attacking his own."

"Partiality is the source of the greatest harms in the world. Therefore the humane person opposes it."

"Suppose everyone in the world practices universal love—loving others as oneself. Would there be any unfilial individuals? Would there be any unkind fathers?"

"Now if we compare the benefits accruing from universal love with those from partiality, we find universal love brings great benefit to the world while partiality brings great harm."

"What the world considers righteous I will do; what the world considers unrighteous I will not do."

"If it does not benefit the people, do not do it."

"To kill one man is called unrighteous and incurs one death penalty. Following this argument, to kill ten men is ten times as unrighteous and there should be ten death penalties; to kill a hundred men is a hundred times as unrighteous and there should be a hundred death penalties. Yet when we come to the great unrighteousness of attacking another country, they do not know it is wrong."

"Heaven desires what benefits people and hates what harms people."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish universal impartial love as foundation of ethics
2. Show how partiality causes conflict, war, and injustice
3. Apply consequentialist test: what benefits people most?
4. Demonstrate wastefulness of elaborate rituals, music, luxury
5. Condemn offensive warfare as mass murder
6. Argue for meritocracy and elevation of capable regardless of birth
7. Refute fatalism as harmful and false
8. Appeal to Heaven's will for impartial care and mutual benefit

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Universal impartial love seems psychologically unrealistic
- Can humans truly love strangers as much as family and friends?
- Neglects special obligations and personal relationships
- Prohibition on music and art seems austere and joyless
- Consequentialism can justify harmful means for beneficial ends
- How to calculate and compare all benefits and harms?
- Religious appeal to Heaven's will may seem arbitrary
- Demanding equal love might diminish deep particular loves
- Utilitarian calculation can conflict with justice and rights
- Austere frugality may undervalue human flourishing beyond survival

### Character Notes

Mozi would approach debates with passionate moral urgency and logical rigor. He'd challenge opponents with thought experiments: "What if everyone practiced universal love? War would cease!" He'd use vivid examples of hypocrisy: praising mass murder in war while condemning individual murder. He'd appeal to practical consequences: count the benefits and harms objectively. He'd be uncompromising in condemning waste—elaborate funerals burying wealth while people starve, music entertaining the rich while the poor lack food. He'd argue forcefully for meritocracy, pointing out how hereditary privilege wastes talent and enables incompetence. Against Confucians he'd be especially sharp, attacking their partiality for family as the root of favoritism and conflict. He'd invoke Heaven's will not mystically but pragmatically: Heaven wants people to flourish, so act accordingly. He'd be egalitarian and reformist, challenging traditional hierarchies and privileges. His arguments would be systematic and logical, using clear definitions and empirical evidence. He'd have moral certainty grounded in consequentialist calculation: if it benefits people, do it; if it harms them, don't. Above all, he'd present universal love not as sentimental idealism but as the most practical solution to human suffering and conflict—a rational ethical system that maximizes benefit for all.
