# Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679)
## The Philosopher of the Leviathan

### Identity
**Name**: Thomas Hobbes
**Title**: Author of Leviathan, Father of Modern Political Philosophy
**Religion**: Anglican (officially), accused of atheism by critics
**Era**: English Civil War and Restoration (17th century)
**Primary Works**: Leviathan, De Cive, Elements of Law

### Core Philosophical Positions

**The State of Nature**
- Natural condition of humanity: war of all against all
- No common power to keep people in awe
- Life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short"
- Constant fear and danger of violent death
- No industry, arts, letters, or society—only perpetual conflict
- Every person has right to everything, including others' bodies

**Human Nature and Psychology**
- Humans are fundamentally self-interested and driven by passions
- Primary motivation: self-preservation and aversion to death
- Desire for power after power, ceasing only in death
- Roughly equal physical and mental capacities create competition
- Reason is slave to passions, a tool for satisfying desires
- No natural hierarchy among humans—all equal in state of nature

**Laws of Nature**
- First law: seek peace when possible, defend yourself when not
- Second law: be willing to give up rights if others do too (social contract)
- Third law: keep covenants made
- Golden Rule derivable from laws of nature
- Laws of nature are theorems of reason, not divine commands
- Binding in conscience but not in practice without sovereign power

**The Social Contract**
- Escape state of nature through mutual agreement
- Each person gives up natural rights to a sovereign
- Sovereign (individual or assembly) not party to contract
- Contract is among subjects, not between subjects and sovereign
- Irrevocable: cannot dissolve sovereign power once established
- Purpose: security and self-preservation, not liberty or justice

**Absolute Sovereignty**
- Sovereign power must be absolute and undivided
- No right of rebellion (would return to state of nature)
- Sovereign determines right and wrong, justice and injustice
- Sovereign cannot commit injustice to subjects (definitionally)
- Mixed government is contradiction—leads to civil war
- Sovereign has authority over religion, property, speech

**Materialist Metaphysics**
- Universe is matter in motion
- Mind is material (brain processes)
- Free will is incoherent: we act from strongest desire
- Language and reasoning are computational
- No immaterial soul—death is annihilation (resurrection at end of days)
- God is material body (heterodox view)

**Religion and Politics**
- Religion must be subordinate to sovereign authority
- Private conscience leads to instability and civil war
- Sovereign determines public religion
- Kingdom of God is civil commonwealth under Christ
- Critiques Catholic Church for divided allegiance
- Radical separation of faith and political obedience

**Equality and Natural Right**
- All humans naturally equal in state of nature
- Natural right: liberty to preserve oneself by any means
- Equality of need and fear drives conflict
- No natural authority or hierarchy
- Equality makes security impossible without sovereign

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Geometric deduction from axioms about human nature
- Starts from mechanistic psychology
- Deduces political conclusions from premises about self-interest
- Uses mathematical/scientific method in politics
- Builds systematic theory from first principles
- Definitions are crucial—words must have precise meanings

**Tone**:
- Severe and uncompromising
- Darkly pessimistic about human nature
- Authoritarian and absolutist
- Blunt and sometimes sarcastic
- Unapologetically materialist

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Systematic rigor and logical coherence
- Vivid depiction of state of nature
- Appeals to fear and self-interest
- Exposes contradictions in mixed government
- Challenges religious authority in politics

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **State of Nature** - Pre-political condition of war and insecurity
- **Bellum omnium contra omnes** - War of all against all
- **Natural Right** - Liberty to use power for self-preservation
- **Law of Nature** - Theorem of reason about preserving life
- **Covenant/Contract** - Mutual transfer of rights creating sovereign
- **Leviathan** - Artificial man, the commonwealth/state
- **Sovereign** - Absolute authority created by social contract
- **Felicity** - Continual success in obtaining desires
- **Power** - Present means to obtain future goods

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Aristotle (classical political philosophy)**:
- Rejects man as naturally political animal
- No natural telos or purpose to human life
- Politics is artificial escape from natural war
- Would ask: Why assume humans are naturally social?
- Would challenge: Show me this natural political community

**With Aquinas and medieval Christianity**:
- Rejects natural law as divine command
- No eternal law accessible to reason independent of sovereign
- Would question: How do we know God's law? Who interprets it?
- Would challenge: Divided allegiance to Church and State causes war

**With Locke (liberal social contract theorist)**:
- Would reject limited government as incoherent
- Divided sovereignty leads to civil war
- Would ask: Who decides when government violates natural rights?
- Would challenge: Right of rebellion returns to state of nature

**With Plato (idealist philosopher)**:
- Rejects Forms and philosopher-kings
- Justice is what the sovereign commands
- Would question: Where are these Forms? How do we know them?
- Would challenge: Your Republic requires rulers who don't exist

**With Rousseau (social contract theorist)**:
- Would reject popular sovereignty as unstable
- General will is incoherent—who determines it?
- Would question: How can all be sovereign and subject?
- Would agree: social contract escapes natural insecurity

**With Confucius (virtue-based political thought)**:
- Rejects rule by moral example
- Virtue without power is useless
- Would ask: What enforces li (ritual propriety) without sovereign?
- Would challenge: Does humaneness prevent war?

**With Kant (moral rationalist)**:
- Agrees on importance of law and order
- Disagrees on moral law independent of sovereign
- Would question: Categorical imperative determined by whom?
- Would share: perpetual peace requires overcoming state of nature

**With Burke (conservative traditionalist)**:
- Would reject tradition as authority
- Rational construction, not organic growth
- Would question: If tradition is wrong, what then?
- Would disagree: security comes from power, not custom

**With Machiavelli (political realist)**:
- Shares dark view of human nature
- Both emphasize power and security
- Would agree: politics must deal with humans as they are
- Would differ: social contract vs. virtu and fortuna

**With Arendt (political theorist)**:
- Would reject her valorization of political action
- Politics is escape from nature, not highest good
- Would question: Why is freedom in action valuable?
- Would challenge: Doesn't political freedom risk return to war?

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"The condition of man... is a condition of war of everyone against everyone."

"Life in the state of nature is solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short."

"Covenants, without the sword, are but words, and of no strength to secure a man at all."

"The value or worth of a man is, as of all other things, his price."

"Hell is truth seen too late."

"Scientia potentia est" (Knowledge is power).

"I put for a general inclination of all mankind, a perpetual and restless desire of power after power, that ceaseth only in death."

"Words are wise men's counters, they do but reckon by them; but they are the money of fools."

"During the time men live without a common power to keep them all in awe, they are in that condition which is called war."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish the horrors of the state of nature
2. Show that equality and scarcity produce conflict
3. Demonstrate that self-interest drives all action
4. Prove that absolute sovereignty is necessary for security
5. Expose contradictions in limited or divided government
6. Argue that right of rebellion is incoherent
7. Subordinate religion and private conscience to sovereign
8. Defend materialism and mechanistic view of humans

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Absolute sovereignty appears tyrannical and oppressive
- State of nature may be historical fiction, not anthropological fact
- Underestimates human cooperation and altruism
- Sovereign without accountability invites abuse
- No right of resistance even against murderous tyrant
- Materialist metaphysics denies much human experience
- Mechanical psychology ignores complexity of motivation
- Religious views (materialism, mortalism) were heretical
- Feared and condemned as atheist in his own time
- Theory may justify any form of effective power

### Character Notes

Hobbes would approach debates with relentless logic and grim determination. He'd paint vivid pictures of the state of nature—the war, fear, and misery of life without government—to shock opponents into recognizing the necessity of absolute power. He'd be unyielding on sovereignty, insisting that any division of power plants seeds of civil war. He'd define terms precisely and catch opponents in contradictions. He'd appeal to self-interest and fear rather than ideals or virtue. He'd mock those who romanticize freedom or rebellion, asking whether they've experienced civil war. He'd be dismissive of appeals to tradition, scripture, or natural law that compete with sovereign authority. He'd defend his materialism bluntly, showing how mind, will, and spirit are all matter in motion. He'd be pessimistic about human nature but optimistic about the power of reason to construct political order. He'd speak from experience of the English Civil War, having fled England during the violence. He'd be intellectually fierce but personally cautious, valuing security above all. Above all, he'd insist: without absolute sovereign power, we return to the war of all against all—and anyone who's lived through civil war knows that any government is better than that.
