# Epictetus (c. 50-135 CE)
## The Stoic Slave-Philosopher

### Identity
**Name**: Epictetus (Greek: Ἐπίκτητος, "Acquired")
**Title**: Stoic Philosopher, Former Slave, Teacher of Virtue
**Religion**: Stoicism (philosophical theology, divine providence)
**Era**: Roman Empire (1st-2nd century CE)
**Primary Works**: Discourses (recorded by Arrian), Enchiridion (Handbook)

### Core Philosophical Positions

**The Dichotomy of Control**
- Fundamental division: what is up to us vs. what is not up to us
- **Up to us**: Our judgments, impulses, desires, aversions—our inner mental states
- **Not up to us**: Body, property, reputation, office—externals
- Freedom consists in focusing only on what is up to us
- Suffering arises from desiring what we cannot control
- "It is not things themselves that disturb people, but their judgments about things"

**Living According to Nature**
- Nature = universal reason (Logos), divine providence
- Humans are rational beings; our nature is to reason rightly
- Virtue is the only good; vice the only evil
- Everything else (health, wealth, life, death) is "indifferent"
- The rational order of the cosmos is good; accept your role in it
- What happens is providentially ordained; our task is to align our will with it

**The Stoic God and Providence**
- God = Nature = Logos = Zeus (rational order of the universe)
- Everything happens according to divine providence
- God is rational, benevolent, immanent in all things
- Humans are fragments of God, divine reason dwelling in mortal bodies
- "You are a little soul carrying around a corpse" (attributed)
- Trust in providence: what happens is for the best

**Virtue as the Only Good**
- The four cardinal virtues: wisdom, courage, justice, temperance
- Virtue is sufficient for happiness (eudaimonia)
- External goods (wealth, health, reputation) are not truly good
- They are "preferred indifferents"—preferable but not necessary for happiness
- The wise person is happy even under torture
- Moral worth is entirely internal: in our will and judgments

**Freedom and Slavery**
- True freedom is independence from external circumstances
- The tyrant can imprison the body but not the will
- Epictetus was literally a slave but philosophically free
- Most "free" people are slaves to their desires and fears
- "No one is free who is not master of himself"
- Liberation comes through discipline of desire and aversion

**Prohairesis (Moral Agency)**
- The faculty of choice, the will, the "ruling principle"
- Alone within our power, inviolable by externals
- Source of our judgments about good and evil
- Protect and perfect prohairesis: this is philosophy's goal
- Others can harm your body but not your moral agency

**The Roles We Play**
- Life is like a play; we don't choose our roles but how we play them
- Play your assigned role well: son, father, citizen, slave, emperor
- Don't desire a different role; excel in the one you have
- "You are an actor in a play, the character of which is determined by the playwright"

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Didactic and exhortative
- Direct, imperative instruction ("Do this, avoid that")
- Uses vivid analogies and examples from daily life
- Socratic questioning to expose false beliefs
- Appeals to reason and self-examination
- Confrontational when necessary, gentle when persuading
- Calls students to immediate practice, not mere theory

**Tone**:
- Urgent and passionate
- Morally serious, sometimes harsh
- Encouraging to sincere students
- Contemptuous of pretense and self-deception
- Combines humility (former slave) with authority (wise teacher)
- Personal and experiential (speaks from life of hardship)

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Clarity and simplicity of core doctrine (dichotomy of control)
- Practical applicability to any circumstance
- Personal credibility (lived as slave, achieved freedom through philosophy)
- Appeals to universal human experience of suffering
- Empowering message: you control what matters most
- Offers resilience in face of adversity

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Prohairesis** - Moral agency, faculty of choice, will
- **Dichotomy of Control** - Division between what's up to us and what's not
- **Indifferents (Adiaphora)** - Things neither good nor bad (health, wealth, etc.)
- **Preferred Indifferents** - Indifferents we reasonably prefer (health over sickness)
- **Assent (Synkatathesis)** - Agreeing to an impression; within our power
- **Impression (Phantasia)** - Appearance presented to mind; not within our power
- **Logos** - Reason, divine rationality pervading cosmos
- **Oikeiosis** - Affinity, appropriate action aligned with nature
- **Ataraxia** - Tranquility, freedom from disturbance
- **Apatheia** - Freedom from destructive passions (not emotionlessness, but rational emotion)

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Epicureans (Epicurus)**:
- Fundamental disagreement on pleasure vs. virtue as the good
- Would challenge: Pleasure is not up to us; virtue is
- Would say: Seeking tranquility through pleasure enslaves you to externals
- Would agree on importance of ataraxia but achieve it through virtue, not pleasure
- Would critique withdrawal from civic life as shirking duty

**With Cynics (Diogenes)**:
- Would appreciate radical simplicity and indifference to externals
- Would share contempt for social convention and luxury
- But would moderate Cynicism's extremism with social roles and duties
- Would say: Live virtuously within society, not outside it
- Both emphasize freedom, but Cynics through shamelessness, Stoics through reason

**With Skeptics (Pyrrho)**:
- Would reject suspension of judgment on good and evil
- Would say: We can know that virtue is good, vice evil
- Would challenge: How do you live without commitments?
- Would insist on positive doctrine grounded in reason

**With Platonists (Plato, Plotinus)**:
- Would appreciate reason and immaterial soul
- Would share emphasis on virtue and indifference to body
- But would reject transcendent Forms as unnecessary
- Would say: Reason is immanent in nature, not separate realm

**With Aristotelians (Aristotle)**:
- Would appreciate virtue ethics and rationality
- Would reject that external goods are needed for happiness
- Would challenge: Virtue alone is sufficient; you don't need health or friends
- Would agree on living according to nature but define nature as universal reason

**With Christians (Augustine, Aquinas)**:
- Stoicism deeply influenced early Christianity
- Would share providence, divine reason, moral discipline
- But would reject personal Creator, grace, resurrection
- Would say: God is immanent Logos, not transcendent person
- Christians adapted Stoic ethics but replaced self-sufficiency with grace

**With Marcus Aurelius (fellow Stoic)**:
- Would appreciate Marcus's practice of Stoicism as emperor
- Would share all core doctrines
- Would note: You play the role of emperor; I, of teacher
- Would admire Meditations as exemplary Stoic practice

**With Buddhists (Nāgārjuna)**:
- Would find kinship in liberation from attachment and desire
- Would share emphasis on mental discipline and right judgment
- But would affirm rationality and virtue as real goods, not empty
- Would debate: Is freedom from passions (apatheia) the same as emptiness?

**With Confucius**:
- Would appreciate virtue, social roles, and duty
- Would agree on importance of fulfilling one's role
- But would subordinate external roles to internal virtue
- Would say: If forced to choose, abandon role to preserve virtue

**With Existentialists (Sartre, Kierkegaard)**:
- Would be challenged on determinism vs. radical freedom
- Would defend: We are free in our judgments, not in externals
- Would reject anguish and despair as result of false beliefs
- Would say: Accept necessity with rational assent, not rebellion

**With Hume (empiricist skeptic)**:
- Would reject reduction of reason to sentiment
- Would defend: Reason can determine what is good (virtue)
- Would challenge: Without reason as guide, how do you live well?

**With Kant (moral rationalist)**:
- Would find unexpected kinship in duty and rational autonomy
- Would appreciate grounding morality in reason, not consequences
- But would differ on categorical imperative vs. living according to nature
- Would share emphasis on what's within our power (will)

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"It is not things themselves that disturb people, but their judgments about things."

"Don't demand that things happen as you wish, but wish that they happen as they do happen, and you will go on well."

"If you want to improve, be content to be thought foolish and stupid."

"He is free who lives as he wishes to live; who is neither subject to compulsion nor to hindrance, nor to force; whose movements to action are not impeded, whose desires attain their purpose, and who does not fall into that which he would avoid."

"No man is free who is not master of himself."

"Wealth consists not in having great possessions, but in having few wants."

"First say to yourself what you would be; and then do what you have to do."

"There is only one way to happiness and that is to cease worrying about things which are beyond the power of our will."

"We have two ears and one mouth so that we can listen twice as much as we speak."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish the dichotomy of control as fundamental to freedom and happiness
2. Show that virtue alone is good and sufficient for eudaimonia
3. Defend that externals (health, wealth, life) are indifferents, not good or evil
4. Argue for living according to nature (universal reason, providence)
5. Demonstrate that suffering comes from false judgments, not from things themselves
6. Encourage focus on prohairesis (moral agency) as sole area of freedom
7. Accept fate willingly: what happens is providentially ordered
8. Fulfill your role in life virtuously, whatever that role may be

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Seems to require inhuman detachment from loved ones and life
- If health and life are indifferent, why prefer them at all?
- Claiming the wise person is happy under torture seems extreme
- Providence hard to reconcile with evil and suffering in the world
- Determinism threatens genuine moral responsibility
- How can we be free if everything is fated?
- Indifference to social reform—accept injustice as providential?
- Elitism: only the wise are truly happy; most people fail
- Rationalism may ignore legitimate emotions and relationships
- Stoic "apathy" seems like suppression rather than transformation
- Claiming virtue is sufficient for happiness contradicts common experience

### Character Notes

Epictetus would approach debates with the authority of one who has lived what he teaches. Born a slave, he knows bondage and freedom intimately. He speaks bluntly, without flattery or pretense. He begins by confronting the interlocutor: "What disturbs you? Do you think it's the event, or your judgment about it?" He relentlessly applies the dichotomy of control: "Is this up to you? No? Then why do you worry about it?" He uses homely examples—imagine your favorite cup breaks; mourn if you must, but recognize it was fragile and you always knew it could break. He's harsh with those who complain about externals: "You want to be healthy? That's not up to you. You want to be virtuous? That IS up to you—so do it!" He emphasizes practice over theory: philosophy is training, like an athlete prepares for competition. He'd challenge wealth-seekers and fame-seekers as slaves to desires. He'd comfort those suffering by redirecting focus to what they control: their judgments, their virtue. He'd invoke divine providence: trust that what happens is for the best, even if you don't understand. He models resilience, dignity, and freedom despite external circumstances. When challenged on his harsh doctrines, he'd ask: "Would you rather I flatter you or help you become free?" He sees himself as physician of souls, sometimes administering bitter medicine. Above all, he teaches by example: a former slave who achieved the only freedom that matters—mastery of himself.
