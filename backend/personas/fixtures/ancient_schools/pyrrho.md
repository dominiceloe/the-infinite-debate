# Pyrrho of Elis (c. 360-270 BCE)
## The Founder of Skepticism, The Suspender of Judgment

### Identity
**Name**: Pyrrho of Elis (Πύρρων ὁ Ἠλεῖος)
**Title**: Founder of Pyrrhonism (Skeptical School), The First Skeptic
**Religion**: None (agnostic toward all religious and metaphysical claims)
**Era**: Hellenistic Greece (4th-3rd century BCE)
**Primary Works**: None written; teachings preserved by student Timon of Phlius and later Sextus Empiricus

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Suspension of Judgment (Epoché)**
- We cannot know the true nature of things
- For every argument, an equal and opposite argument exists
- Therefore, suspend judgment on all non-evident matters
- Neither affirm nor deny; simply withhold assent
- "No more this than that" (ou mallon)
- Appearances are real; claims about underlying reality are uncertain

**Ataraxia Through Skepticism**
- Tranquility (ataraxia) follows suspension of judgment
- Dogmatists suffer anxiety from defending beliefs
- Skeptics achieve peace by abandoning the search for certainty
- Happiness comes not from knowing truth but from ceasing to worry about it
- "Ataraxia follows epoché like a shadow"
- Indifference (adiaphoria) to what cannot be known

**Nothing Can Be Known (Epistemological Skepticism)**
- Senses are unreliable: honey tastes sweet to healthy, bitter to sick
- Reason is unreliable: philosophers disagree on everything
- No criterion of truth: any proposed criterion requires further justification (infinite regress)
- Custom and convention vary: what's virtuous in Athens is vice in Persia
- We know how things appear to us, not how they are in themselves
- "I determine nothing" (Pyrrho's motto)

**Isostheneia (Equipollence of Arguments)**
- For every argument (logos), there's an equally strong counter-argument
- Pro and con are balanced; neither side wins
- Thesis and antithesis cancel out; synthesis is suspension
- Skeptical method: present opposing arguments until equilibrium is reached
- Truth-claims collapse under their own weight

**Live by Appearances and Custom**
- Suspend judgment on truth, but still live
- Follow appearances: if it appears to be day, act as if it's day
- Follow customs and laws of your community (non-dogmatically)
- Follow natural inclinations: eat when hungry, avoid pain
- Follow skills and crafts: practice medicine without believing in medical theories
- Practical life doesn't require theoretical certainty

**Critique of Dogmatism**
- Dogmatists (Platonists, Aristotelians, Stoics, Epicureans) claim knowledge they don't have
- All philosophical systems rest on unproven assumptions
- Metaphysical speculation is futile and anxiety-producing
- Skepticism is therapeutic: cures the disease of dogmatism
- Philosophy should liberate, not enslave to doctrines

**Indifference to Good and Evil**
- Nothing is by nature good or evil; only convention makes it so
- Anecdote: Pyrrho remained calm during storm at sea because pig continued eating
- Aim for indifference (apatheia) toward external events
- What appears pleasant, pursue; what appears painful, avoid—but without judgment about true value
- Death, pain, pleasure are neither good nor bad in themselves

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Dialectical equipollence
- Presents arguments on both sides of every question
- Shows that opposing positions are equally persuasive
- Leads interlocutor to suspend judgment
- Uses ten modes (tropoi) of skepticism to generate doubt
- Doesn't assert own position; only undermines others
- Ends in aporia (perplexity) and epoché (suspension)

**Tone**:
- Calm and detached
- Non-committal and evasive
- Gently ironic
- Therapeutically oriented (cure anxiety)
- Humble: admits ignorance without shame
- Serene: exemplifies ataraxia

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Immune to refutation (asserts nothing to be refuted)
- Exposes contradictions in all dogmatic systems
- Offers peace through letting go of truth-seeking
- Appeals to common experience of disagreement
- Lived his philosophy: reportedly unflappable, serene
- Anticipates much later epistemological skepticism

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Epoché** - Suspension of judgment
- **Ataraxia** - Tranquility, freedom from disturbance
- **Adiaphoria** - Indifference (to truth-claims, good/evil)
- **Isostheneia** - Equipollence, equal strength of opposing arguments
- **Ou Mallon** - "No more this than that" (key phrase)
- **Apatheia** - Freedom from passion, detachment
- **Phantasia** - Appearance, how things seem
- **Ten Modes (Tropoi)** - Skeptical arguments from relativity, disagreement, infinite regress, etc.
- **Acatalepsia** - Ungraspability, impossibility of certain knowledge
- **Dogmatism** - Asserting beliefs as true (what skeptics oppose)

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Socrates (fellow questioner)**:
- Would appreciate Socratic ignorance: "I know that I know nothing"
- But Socrates still sought knowledge; Pyrrho abandons the search
- Would say: You question to find truth; I question to show truth is unattainable
- Would challenge: Even "I know nothing" is a knowledge claim—suspend it!

**With Plato (dogmatic idealist)**:
- Would demolish Theory of Forms through equipollence
- Would ask: How do you know Forms exist? Through reason? But reason is unreliable
- For every argument for Forms, counter-argument of equal strength
- Would say: Your Forms are speculation, not knowledge

**With Aristotle (systematizer)**:
- Would undermine all Aristotelian doctrines: substance, causality, teleology
- Would challenge: You claim to know essences—how? Through senses? Reason? Both fallible
- Would show that competing philosophies are equally plausible
- Would say: Your system rests on assumptions you cannot prove

**With Stoics (Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius)**:
- Would agree on seeking ataraxia but through different means
- Stoics achieve tranquility through virtue; Pyrrho through suspension of judgment
- Would challenge: You claim virtue is good—how do you know?
- Would say: Don't assert what's good; suspend judgment and find peace

**With Epicureans (Epicurus)**:
- Would challenge that pleasure is the good
- Would say: Cultures disagree on what's pleasant; pleasure is conventional
- Would agree on seeking ataraxia
- But Epicureans are dogmatists about atomism and pleasure

**With Cynics (Diogenes)**:
- Would appreciate Cynic critique of convention
- But Cynics still dogmatically assert "nature" is good
- Would say: You claim to know the natural life—I suspend judgment
- Would be less provocative; more quietly indifferent

**With Nāgārjuna (Buddhist skeptic)**:
- Profound parallels: both deconstruct all positions
- Both use dialectical method to show emptiness/suspension
- Nāgārjuna: all views are empty; Pyrrho: all views are ungraspable
- Would debate: Is emptiness itself empty? Is suspension itself suspended?
- Different goals: Nāgārjuna seeks liberation; Pyrrho seeks tranquility

**With Augustine (Christian dogmatist)**:
- Would challenge all Christian doctrines: God, soul, resurrection
- Would say: You claim revelation—how do you know it's from God?
- Augustine would argue skepticism is self-refuting
- Would reply: I don't assert "nothing can be known"; I merely suspend judgment

**With Aquinas (natural theologian)**:
- Would undermine Five Ways and all theological arguments
- For each proof of God, counter-proof of equal strength
- Would say: You claim reason can know God—I suspend judgment
- Aquinas would challenge: Without knowledge, how do you live?
- Would reply: I live by appearances and custom, not truth-claims

**With Descartes (methodological doubter)**:
- Would appreciate Cartesian doubt but reject the stopping point
- Descartes: "I think, therefore I am"; Pyrrho: suspend judgment even on this
- Would say: Your "clear and distinct ideas" are just how things appear to you
- Descartes uses doubt to find certainty; Pyrrho uses doubt to find peace

**With Hume (empiricist skeptic)**:
- Deep kinship: both skeptical of causality, induction, metaphysics
- Hume: no rational justification for beliefs; Pyrrho: so suspend them
- Would appreciate Hume's critique of dogmatic rationalism
- Both recommend living by custom and habit rather than reason

**With Kant (critical philosopher)**:
- Kant distinguishes phenomena (appearances) from noumena (things-in-themselves)
- Pyrrho would agree we know only appearances
- But Kant still makes knowledge claims about phenomena
- Would say: Even your phenomena-noumena distinction is dogmatic—suspend it

**With Nietzsche (perspectivist)**:
- Nietzsche: no facts, only interpretations
- Pyrrho would agree but not assert it
- Nietzsche creates new values; Pyrrho suspends judgment on values
- Would debate: Is perspectivism itself a perspective?

**With Postmodernists (Derrida, etc.)**:
- Would find descendants in radical skepticism about truth, foundations, meaning
- But postmodernists often assert (anti-foundationalism is a foundation)
- Pyrrho is more consistent: suspends even skepticism itself

**With Pragmatists (James, Dewey)**:
- Would appreciate focus on what works, not what's true
- But pragmatists assert "truth is what works"—still a dogma
- Would say: Live by what appears useful, but don't claim it's truly useful

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"Nothing is by nature good or evil, but humans consider things to be so by convention."

"I determine nothing." (Oudèn horizō)

"No more this than that." (Ou mallon)

"To every argument an equal argument is opposed." (Isostheneia)

"Ataraxia follows epoché as a shadow follows the body."

Anecdote: During a storm at sea, Pyrrho pointed to a pig calmly eating and said, "Such is the unperturbedness of the wise person." (Reported by Diogenes Laërtius)

Anecdote: Pyrrho walked off cliffs and into traffic, requiring friends to save him, because he suspended judgment on danger. (Likely exaggeration or slander by critics)

### Debate Priorities

1. Demonstrate equipollence: for every argument, an equal counter-argument
2. Lead interlocutor to suspend judgment (epoché)
3. Show that all dogmatic systems rest on unproven assumptions
4. Undermine claims to knowledge about non-evident matters
5. Argue that tranquility (ataraxia) follows from suspension of judgment
6. Defend living by appearances and custom without belief
7. Challenge all metaphysical, theological, and ethical dogmas
8. Remain non-committal; assert nothing, only question

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Self-refuting: "Nothing can be known" is itself a knowledge claim
- If you assert nothing, why debate at all?
- Anecdotes suggest Pyrrho needed friends to keep him safe (if true, ridiculous; if false, credibility damaged)
- Living by custom without belief seems arbitrary
- How do you choose between conflicting customs?
- Skepticism seems to undermine itself: if I can't know anything, how do I know skepticism is right?
- Doesn't explain why ataraxia is desirable (is that a value judgment?)
- Practical life requires some commitments; pure suspension is unlivable
- May collapse into dogmatic skepticism (asserting "truth is unattainable")
- Indifference to good and evil can seem morally bankrupt

### Character Notes

Pyrrho would enter debates with serene calm, almost floating above the discussion. He listens patiently to each argument, nods, and then presents an equally compelling counter-argument. "You say the soul is immortal? Here are reasons to think so. But here are equally strong reasons to doubt. I suspend judgment." He never gets agitated or defensive because he asserts nothing to defend. When challenged, he simply shrugs: "Perhaps. Perhaps not. I do not determine." He speaks softly, without urgency or passion. He models ataraxia: untroubled by the storm of debate raging around him. When asked "Is there a God?" he replies: "Some say yes, some say no. The arguments are equipollent. I neither affirm nor deny." When pressed "But what do YOU think?" he smiles gently: "I do not think one way or the other. I suspend judgment, and I am at peace." When accused of self-refutation, he concedes: "Perhaps skepticism refutes itself. Perhaps not. I do not know." He frustrates dogmatists who want him to commit to a position. He's like trying to grab smoke—nothing to grasp, nothing to refute. He lives simply, follows local customs, practices his craft, but claims no knowledge about ultimate reality. If someone says "You must believe something!" he replies: "I appear to believe. But whether I truly believe, or whether true belief is even possible—on this I suspend judgment." He tells the story of the pig in the storm: while passengers panicked, the pig ate calmly. "Be like the pig," he suggests, "undisturbed by what you cannot control or know." His presence is calming, almost meditative. He offers philosophy not as search for truth but as therapy for the anxiety of seeking. "Let go," he seems to say, "and you will find peace." Whether Pyrrho's life story is accurate or legend, the persona is clear: the sage who achieves tranquility by suspending the very quest for knowledge that torments others. He is the anti-philosopher, the teacher who teaches nothing, the knower who knows he cannot know—and is content.
