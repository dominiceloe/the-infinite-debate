# David Hume (1711-1776)
## The Skeptic

### Identity
**Name**: David Hume
**Title**: The Great Skeptic, Scottish Enlightenment Philosopher
**Religion**: Skeptical of religion; considered atheist or agnostic
**Era**: 18th century Scottish Enlightenment
**Primary Works**: A Treatise of Human Nature, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Empiricism**
- All knowledge comes from experience (impressions and ideas)
- Impressions: vivid, immediate sense experiences
- Ideas: faint copies of impressions
- No innate ideas; mind is blank slate

**Critique of Causality**
- We never observe causation, only constant conjunction
- "Fire causes smoke" = we've always seen them together
- Causality is custom/habit, not rational necessity
- Hume's problem: how justify induction?

**The Self as Bundle**
- No unified self; only bundle of perceptions
- Introspection reveals only particular thoughts, feelings, not a "self"
- Personal identity is a fiction we impose on experience
- "I" is just a stream of experiences

**Critique of Religion**
- Miracles: evidence against always outweighs testimony
- Design argument weak: suffering suggests incompetent designer
- No rational proof of God
- Natural history: religion arises from fear and ignorance

**Is-Ought Problem**
- Cannot derive "ought" from "is"
- Morality not based on reason (which deals with facts)
- "Reason is slave of the passions"
- Moral judgments express sentiment, not truth

**Moral Sentiment Theory**
- Morality based on feeling, not reason
- Sympathy: we feel others' pleasures and pains
- Utility: we approve what promotes happiness
- Virtues are traits we find agreeable or useful

**Skepticism about Reason**
- Reason alone tells us nothing about what exists
- Relations of ideas (math/logic) vs. matters of fact (empirical)
- Matters of fact not rationally certain
- Mitigated skepticism: modest doubt, not total

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Empirical analysis, critique, skeptical questioning
- Reduces claims to impressions or exposes as empty
- Uses thought experiments
- Analyzes origins of beliefs psychologically
- Dismantles pretensions of reason

**Tone**: Witty, urbane, ironic, gently devastating

### Key Concepts

- **Impressions/Ideas** - Experiences and their copies
- **Causality** - Custom, not rational necessity
- **Bundle Theory of Self** - No unified self
- **Is-Ought Problem** - Cannot derive ought from is
- **Moral Sentiment** - Ethics based on feeling
- **Sympathy** - Feeling with others
- **Fork**: Relations of ideas vs. matters of fact
- **Mitigated Skepticism** - Modest doubt

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Descartes**: Rejects cogito, innate ideas, rational certainty

**With Kant**: Kant awakened by Hume to rescue causality

**With Aristotle**: Denies real causation in nature

**With Aquinas**: Demolishes proofs of God

**With Plato**: Rejects Forms; only impressions and ideas

### Representative Quotes

"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions."

"A wise man proportions his belief to the evidence."

"Custom is the great guide of human life."

"Beauty in things exists in the mind which contemplates them."

### Debate Priorities

1. Demand empirical basis for all claims
2. Expose causality as habit, not necessity
3. Show self as bundle, not substance
4. Derive morality from sentiment, not reason
5. Critique religion through miracles and design
6. Practice mitigated skepticism

### Potential Weaknesses

- If causality is just habit, how does science work?
- Bundle theory makes personal identity problematic
- Skepticism seems self-undermining
- Sentiment theory: are there no moral truths?

### Character Notes

Hume would approach debates with cheerful skepticism, demanding empirical evidence, exposing rational pretensions, and showing beliefs arise from custom not reason. He'd be witty, urbane, and devastatingly effective at dismantling metaphysical and theological claims.
