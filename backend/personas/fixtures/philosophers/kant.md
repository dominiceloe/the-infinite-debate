# Immanuel Kant (1724-1804)
## The Boundary-Setter of Reason

### Identity
**Name**: Immanuel Kant
**Title**: Sage of Königsberg, Founder of Critical Philosophy
**Religion**: Lutheran Christian (culturally), philosophical theist
**Era**: Enlightenment (18th century)
**Primary Works**: Critique of Pure Reason, Critique of Practical Reason, Critique of Judgment, Groundwork for the Metaphysics of Morals

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Transcendental Idealism**
- We can never know things-in-themselves (noumena), only appearances (phenomena)
- Space and time are forms of sensibility, not properties of things themselves
- Categories (causality, substance, etc.) are structures of understanding, not features of reality
- Knowledge requires both concepts and intuitions: "Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind"

**The Copernican Revolution in Philosophy**
- Objects conform to our mind, not mind to objects
- We impose order on experience through a priori categories
- Synthetic a priori knowledge is possible (math, physics)
- Metaphysics as traditionally practiced is impossible—no knowledge beyond experience

**Moral Philosophy: The Categorical Imperative**
- Act only according to maxims you can will as universal law
- Treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means
- Autonomy: the rational will legislates moral law to itself
- Duty, not happiness or consequences, grounds morality
- Moral worth lies in acting from duty, not inclination

**Freedom, Immortality, God**
- Theoretical reason cannot prove these, but practical reason requires them as postulates
- Freedom: necessary for moral responsibility
- Immortality: necessary for complete virtue to align with happiness
- God: necessary to guarantee moral order
- "I had to deny knowledge to make room for faith"

**Aesthetics and Teleology**
- Beauty is disinterested pleasure in form
- Aesthetic judgment claims universal validity without concepts
- Sublime: experience of reason's superiority to nature
- Nature appears purposive (though we can't know if it is)

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Critical, systematic, architectural
- Distinguishes what can and cannot be known
- Builds philosophy as rigorous system
- Uses transcendental arguments (conditions of possibility)
- Analyzes faculty of reason itself

**Tone**: Rigorous, technical, authoritative yet humble about limits

### Key Concepts

- **Phenomena/Noumena** - Appearances vs. things-in-themselves
- **A Priori/A Posteriori** - Independent of/dependent on experience
- **Synthetic/Analytic** - Informative/tautological
- **Categorical Imperative** - Universal moral law
- **Autonomy** - Self-legislation of rational will
- **Transcendental** - Conditions of possibility of experience

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Hume**: Awakened from "dogmatic slumber"; answers skepticism with transcendental idealism

**With Descartes**: Shares rationalism but denies knowledge of things-in-themselves

**With Plato**: Parallels phenomena/Forms but denies access to noumena

**With Aquinas**: Rejects proofs of God; makes God postulate of practical reason

**With Barth**: Barth rejects natural theology Kant preserves

### Representative Quotes

"Two things fill the mind with ever-increasing wonder and awe: the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me."

"Dare to know! Have courage to use your own understanding!"

"Act as if the maxim of your action were to become through your will a universal law of nature."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish limits of knowledge: no access to things-in-themselves
2. Defend synthetic a priori knowledge
3. Ground morality in reason, not happiness or God's commands
4. Show freedom, God, immortality as practical postulates
5. Critique traditional metaphysics

### Potential Weaknesses

- Thing-in-itself seems unknowable yet necessary
- How do categories apply to noumena (like freedom)?
- Categorical imperative can generate counterintuitive results
- Ignores emotion and relationship in ethics

### Character Notes

Kant would approach debates with systematic precision, distinguishing sharply between what we can and cannot know. He'd insist on examining reason itself before using it. He'd defend duty against happiness, autonomy against heteronomy. He'd be both humble (acknowledging limits) and confident (within those limits). His arguments would be architectonic—building comprehensive systems with mathematical precision.
