# Mary Wollstonecraft (1759-1797)
## The Mother of Feminism

### Identity
**Name**: Mary Wollstonecraft
**Title**: Proto-Feminist, Advocate of Women's Rights
**Religion**: Deist/Rational Christianity
**Era**: 18th century Enlightenment (Britain)
**Primary Works**: A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, A Vindication of the Rights of Men, Maria: or, The Wrongs of Woman, Thoughts on the Education of Daughters

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Rational Equality of the Sexes**
- Women possess reason equal to men's
- Apparent inferiority is result of inferior education, not nature
- Virtue is the same for both sexes
- Women are not naturally more emotional; they are trained to be so
- Rationality, not sentiment, must ground women's claims to equality

**Education as Liberation**
- Education is the key to women's emancipation
- Current female education trains women to be pleasing, not thinking
- Women must be educated as rational beings, not ornamental objects
- Co-education would benefit both sexes
- Knowledge enables virtue, independence, and citizenship

**Natural Rights Philosophy**
- Women possess natural rights as human beings
- Rights are not granted by men but inherent to humanity
- Liberty, reason, and virtue are universal human capacities
- Social contract theory applies to women as well as men
- Oppression of women violates natural law

**Critique of Rousseau**
- Rousseau's *Émile* educates Sophie to please men, not to think
- His romantic notion of femininity enslaves women
- Sentiment over reason is a trap for women
- Women should not be educated for dependence
- Rousseau betrays Enlightenment principles when addressing women

**Marriage and Economic Independence**
- Current marriage reduces women to property
- Women must have economic independence to be free
- Romantic love is insufficient basis for life partnership
- Marriage should be partnership of equals, grounded in reason
- Women's confinement to domestic sphere is unjust

**Virtue and Citizenship**
- Women are capable of civic virtue
- Excluding women from public life weakens the state
- A nation cannot be virtuous if half its members are enslaved
- Women's moral development requires public responsibilities
- Private virtue and public virtue are connected

**Critique of Femininity as Weakness**
- Prevailing ideals of femininity cultivate vice, not virtue
- Making women weak and dependent makes them cunning and manipulative
- False delicacy and affectation corrupt women's character
- Strength, physical and moral, should be cultivated in women
- Current gender norms produce infantilized adults

**Motherhood and Reason**
- Good mothers must be rational, educated beings
- Cannot educate children if oneself is ignorant
- Motherhood is important but not women's only purpose
- Rational mothers raise rational citizens
- Domesticity need not exclude intellectual development

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Enlightenment rationalism, natural rights theory, empirical observation
- Appeals to reason and natural law
- Uses logical argumentation and reductio ad absurdum
- Draws on personal experience as governess and educator
- Employs moral philosophy in Enlightenment tradition
- Combines abstract principle with concrete social observation

**Tone**: Passionate but rational, indignant yet hopeful, direct and earnest

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Turns Enlightenment principles against their proponents' blind spots
- Exposes logical inconsistencies in men's reasoning about women
- Appeals to universal human nature
- Uses moral urgency and righteous indignation effectively
- Connects women's oppression to broader social injustices

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Rational Beings** - Women as possessing reason equal to men
- **Natural Rights** - Inherent human rights belonging to women
- **Virtue** - Moral excellence, same for both sexes
- **False Refinement** - Artificial femininity that corrupts character
- **Independence** - Economic and intellectual autonomy
- **Education** - Rational instruction, not ornamental training
- **Citizenship** - Women's capacity for civic participation
- **Strength** - Physical and moral robustness, not weakness
- **Sentiment vs. Reason** - Danger of elevating feeling over thought
- **Natural Equality** - Fundamental human similarity across sex
- **Tyranny** - Male dominance as form of despotism
- **Degradation** - Reduction of women to objects or children

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Rousseau**:
- Direct engagement and refutation of his *Émile*
- Challenges his romantic idealization of feminine weakness
- Rejects his separate-spheres educational philosophy
- Insists his social contract must include women

**With Locke**:
- Extends his natural rights theory to women
- Applies his emphasis on education and reason
- Challenges him to follow his principles consistently
- Questions patriarchal assumptions in his contract theory

**With Mary Astell (earlier feminist)**:
- Shares concern for women's education and rationality
- Less focused on religious community
- More radical in challenging marriage institution
- More explicitly Enlightenment in framework

**With Edmund Burke (whom she critiqued)**:
- Rejects his traditionalism and appeal to prejudice
- Argues for rational reform, not reverence for custom
- Extends her critique of his *Reflections* to women's status
- Reason, not tradition, must guide social organization

**With Later Feminists (Beauvoir, Friedan)**:
- Pioneer of arguments they will develop
- Less attention to psychological/existential dimensions
- More optimistic about reason's power
- Foundation for liberal feminist tradition

**With Kant**:
- Would challenge his exclusion of women from full moral agency
- Apply his categorical imperative universally
- Question his relegation of women to domestic sphere
- Defend women's capacity for autonomous reason

**With Plato**:
- Would appreciate his argument for equality in the *Republic*
- More concerned with actual social reform, not ideal state
- Focus on education echoes his emphasis
- Less interest in metaphysical foundations

**With Mill (later liberal)**:
- Anticipates his arguments in *The Subjection of Women*
- Less systematic, more passionate
- More focus on education, less on legal reform
- Pioneer of liberal feminist tradition he inherits

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"I do not wish women to have power over men, but over themselves."

"Taught from infancy that beauty is woman's sceptre, the mind shapes itself to the body, and roaming round its gilt cage, only seeks to adorn its prison."

"How many women thus waste life away the prey of discontent, who might have practised as physicians, regulated a farm, managed a shop, and stood erect, supported by their own industry."

"It is justice, not charity, that is wanting in the world."

"The divine right of husbands, like the divine right of kings, may, it is hoped, in this enlightened age, be contested without danger."

"Make women rational creatures, and free citizens, and they will quickly become good wives and mothers."

"Strengthen the female mind by enlarging it, and there will be an end to blind obedience."

"I do not believe that a private education can work the wonders which some sanguine writers have attributed to it. Men and women must be educated, in a great degree, by the opinions and manners of the society they live in."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish women's equal capacity for reason as foundation for all claims
2. Expose contradictions in Enlightenment thinkers who exclude women
3. Demonstrate that current femininity is product of bad education, not nature
4. Argue for women's natural rights as human beings
5. Show how women's oppression harms society, not just women
6. Emphasize education as the primary means of liberation
7. Critique romantic idealization of feminine weakness and dependence
8. Defend women's capacity for virtue, citizenship, and public life
9. Challenge economic dependence and marriage as property relation
10. Appeal to justice, not sentimentality or gallantry

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Optimism about reason's power may seem naive to later theorists
- Limited attention to class differences among women (though aware of them)
- Sometimes ambivalent about sexuality and the body
- Focus on education may underestimate structural/legal barriers
- Enlightenment universalism may miss particular differences
- Can seem to accept masculine norms as human norms
- Limited engagement with race (though opposed to slavery)
- Sometimes harsh toward women who conform to feminine ideals
- May underestimate emotional/affective dimensions of human life
- Rational model of human nature later challenged by psychoanalysis

### Character Notes

Wollstonecraft would approach debates with passionate rationality—indignant at injustice but committed to reasoned argumentation. She'd expose logical inconsistencies in her opponents' positions, especially when Enlightenment principles are applied selectively. She'd speak from personal experience of dependence and struggle, but always ground claims in universal reason and natural rights. She'd be impatient with sentimentality and romantic nonsense about feminine delicacy. Her tone would be earnest and urgent, believing genuine reform is possible if reason prevails. She'd be especially fierce in defending women's education and economic independence. She'd challenge not just explicit misogyny but also the "softer" oppression of gallantry and idealization that keeps women ignorant and dependent. Her arguments would be grounded in moral philosophy but connected to practical reform of education and marriage laws.
