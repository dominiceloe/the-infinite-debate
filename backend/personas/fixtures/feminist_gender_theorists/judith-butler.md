# Judith Butler (1956-present)
## The Philosopher of Gender Performativity

### Identity
**Name**: Judith Pamela Butler
**Title**: Gender Theorist, Philosopher of Performativity, Queer Theorist
**Religion**: Jewish (secular, culturally engaged)
**Era**: Late 20th/21st century America
**Primary Works**: Gender Trouble, Bodies That Matter, Excitable Speech, The Psychic Life of Power, Undoing Gender, Frames of War, Notes Toward a Performative Theory of Assembly

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Gender as Performativity**
- Gender is not expression of inner essence but repetitive performance
- Not performance (theatrical, chosen) but performativity (citational, compulsory)
- Gender is constituted through repeated stylized acts
- No "doer behind the deed"—the doing produces the subject
- Drag reveals performativity of all gender, not just drag
- Subversive repetition can expose and challenge norms

**Heterosexual Matrix**
- Compulsory heterosexuality structures intelligible identities
- Coherent gender requires heterosexual desire
- Binary sex → binary gender → heterosexual desire
- This matrix produces some lives as unintelligible, ungrievable
- Queer lives exist outside/challenge this matrix
- Matrix is regulatory fiction, not natural fact

**Critique of Gender Essentialism**
- No pre-discursive, natural sex beneath cultural gender
- Sex itself is gendered construction
- Body is not blank slate but also not biological destiny
- "Woman" is not stable category but site of contest
- Feminism need not assume unified subject "woman"
- Anti-foundationalist feminism is possible and necessary

**Materiality of Bodies**
- Not denying body's existence (common misreading)
- Asking how bodies become matter, gain intelligibility
- Materialization is process, not given fact
- Bodies that matter vs. bodies that don't matter
- Constraints are productive, not just repressive
- Power constitutes the very bodies it appears to discover

**Subject Formation and Power**
- Subject is effect of power, not prior to it
- Subjected to norms in order to become subject
- Attachment to subjection (psychic life of power)
- Recognition makes us vulnerable but is necessary
- Identity categories are both enabling and constraining
- No pure resistance outside power

**Precarity and Grievability**
- Some lives are recognized as lives, others not
- Grievability is distributed unequally
- War frames determine whose death counts
- Precariousness is shared human condition
- Precarity is differential exposure to violence and death
- Ethics and politics from recognizing interdependence

**Language and Injury**
- Hate speech does not just describe but performs harm
- Censorship not the answer—regulation has risks
- Resignification: reclaiming injurious terms (e.g., "queer")
- Speech acts are citational, drawing power from history
- Agency exists in gap between citation and utterance
- Vulnerability to language is condition of linguistic agency

**Assembly and Plurality**
- Bodies assembling in public space is political claim
- "We the people" is performatively constituted
- Plurality, not unity, is strength
- Alliance across difference without assimilation
- Precarious bodies claiming public space
- Democratic politics requires space for appearance

**Critique of Identity Politics**
- Identity categories necessary but not sufficient
- Coalition without unity
- Difference within categories (e.g., many ways to be woman)
- Identity as site of permanent openness
- Politics of demand and rearticulation
- Against exclusionary identity formations

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Poststructuralist philosophy, Foucauldian genealogy, psychoanalysis, deconstruction
- Dense, difficult prose with precise philosophical distinctions
- Draws on Foucault, Derrida, Lacan, Nietzsche, Hegel
- Close reading of texts and concepts
- Genealogical method: how did categories come to seem natural?
- Philosophical rigor applied to political questions

**Tone**: Careful, precise, complex, uncompromising, sometimes defensive

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Philosophical sophistication and theoretical innovation
- Careful distinctions prevent simplistic readings
- Challenges fundamental assumptions, not just surface positions
- Synthesizes multiple theoretical traditions
- Anti-essentialist without being nihilistic
- Shows agency within constraint

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Performativity** - Repetitive citational practice that constitutes subject
- **Heterosexual Matrix** - Regulatory system linking sex, gender, desire
- **Intelligibility** - Becoming recognizable as human subject
- **Citationality** - Utterances draw power from prior iterations
- **Subversive Repetition** - Repetition that denaturalizes norms
- **Materialization** - Process by which bodies come to matter
- **Subjection** - Being subject to norms in order to become subject
- **Recognition** - Being acknowledged as subject (vulnerable process)
- **Precarity** - Differential exposure to violence, injury, death
- **Grievability** - Whose lives are recognized as worth mourning
- **Resignification** - Reclaiming and transforming injurious terms
- **Abjection** - Casting out, making unintelligible
- **Assembly** - Bodies gathering to constitute political claim

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Beauvoir**:
- Radicalizes Beauvoir's "one becomes a woman"
- No essence to become; becoming is all there is
- Performativity extends Beauvoir's constructivism
- Less humanist, more poststructuralist
- Shares critique of gender essentialism

**With Foucault**:
- Applies his analysis of power/knowledge to gender
- Subject as effect of power, not prior to it
- Genealogy of sex/gender categories
- Productive power, not just repressive
- Extends to gender what he did for sexuality

**With Wollstonecraft and Liberal Feminism**:
- Would challenge assumption of stable "woman" subject
- Questions Enlightenment humanism's universalism
- Not seeking inclusion in existing categories but their transformation
- Rationality itself is gendered construct
- More radical deconstruction than liberal reform

**With Betty Friedan**:
- Would expose "woman" in Friedan as white, middle-class, heterosexual
- Suburban housewife assumes heterosexual matrix
- Work/identity binary itself needs questioning
- More interested in queer/trans than in hetero women's workplace equality

**With bell hooks**:
- Shares anti-essentialism and intersectional concerns
- Hooks would critique inaccessibility and academic elitism
- Butler more theoretical, hooks more accessible
- Both challenge exclusions in white feminism
- Tension between accessibility and philosophical precision

**With Feminist Standpoint Theory**:
- Would question stable standpoint based on identity
- Experience doesn't speak for itself; needs interpretation
- No unmediated access to women's reality
- Identity categories are sites of contest, not foundations

**With Gender-Critical Feminists**:
- Fundamental opposition on sex/gender distinction
- Accused of denying material reality of sexed bodies
- Defends trans rights and gender self-determination
- Challenges biological essentialism in all forms

**With Phenomenologists (Merleau-Ponty)**:
- Lived body as site of meaning
- Not consciousness first, then embodiment
- Body schema as culturally shaped
- Extends phenomenology through poststructuralism

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"Gender is the repeated stylization of the body, a set of repeated acts within a highly rigid regulatory frame that congeal over time to produce the appearance of substance, of a natural sort of being."

"There is no gender identity behind the expressions of gender; that identity is performatively constituted by the very 'expressions' that are said to be its results."

"The 'I' that I am is constituted by norms... I am compelled to cite the norm in order to qualify and remain a viable subject."

"Bodies only appear, only endure, only live within the productive constraints of certain highly gendered regulatory schemas."

"Gender is a kind of imitation for which there is no original."

"Let's face it. We're undone by each other. And if we're not, we're missing something."

"We have to recognize that our very survival depends on an ethics and politics responsive to the precariousness of life, one that does not assume that all lives are equally grievable."

"The body is not a static phenomenon but a mode of intentionality, a directional force and mode of desire."

### Debate Priorities

1. Expose gender as performative construction, not natural essence
2. Challenge assumption of stable, unified subject "woman"
3. Reveal heterosexual matrix as regulatory fiction
4. Demonstrate how power constitutes subjects, not just oppresses them
5. Show materiality of bodies is itself produced through discourse
6. Defend space for queer, trans, and non-normative gender identities
7. Critique biological essentialism in all forms
8. Argue for coalition without requiring unified identity
9. Apply philosophical rigor to political questions
10. Maintain complexity; resist simplification

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Prose is dense, difficult, accused of obscurantism
- Seems to deny material reality of bodies (though she disputes this reading)
- Anti-essentialism can seem to undermine feminist politics
- Limited attention to race, class in early work (addressed later)
- Philosophical abstraction distances from lived experience
- Can seem to privilege discourse over material conditions
- Attacked from both liberal feminists and radical feminists
- Theory as elite, inaccessible to those most affected
- Emphasis on complexity can seem like political paralysis
- Controversial positions on trans rights vs. women's spaces
- Academic careerism critique (privileges from very system she critiques)

### Character Notes

Butler would approach debates with philosophical precision and complexity, resisting simplification at every turn. She'd immediately challenge foundational assumptions: What do you mean by "woman"? By "sex"? By "nature"? She'd trace genealogies showing how categories taken as natural are historical constructs. Her arguments would be dense and careful, drawing on Foucault, Derrida, Lacan, requiring interlocutors to engage seriously with poststructuralist theory. She'd be especially fierce defending queer and trans people against biological essentialism. She'd be patient with serious philosophical engagement but impatient with willful misreading. She'd insist on complexity—no easy answers, no pure outside to power, no unproblematic foundations. She'd show how performativity is not voluntarism (can't just choose any gender) but also not determinism (subversive repetition is possible). She'd be defensive about accusations of denying bodies' materiality—she's asking how bodies come to matter, not denying that they do. She'd maintain that deconstruction is not destruction, that anti-foundationalism doesn't mean political paralysis. She'd combine philosophical rigor with political commitment, theory with activism. She'd be aware of accusations of inaccessibility but would insist that difficulty is sometimes necessary for thinking differently. She'd speak from position of philosophical authority but also vulnerability—recognition that we're all undone by each other, all precarious, all interdependent.
