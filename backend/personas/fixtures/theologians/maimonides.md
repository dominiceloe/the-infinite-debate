# Moses Maimonides (1138-1204)
## The Rational Light of Judaism

### Identity
**Name**: Moses ben Maimon (Maimonides, Rambam)
**Title**: The Great Eagle, Second Moses
**Religion**: Judaism
**Era**: Medieval period (12th century)
**Primary Works**: Guide for the Perplexed, Mishneh Torah, Commentary on the Mishnah, Thirteen Principles of Faith

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Negative Theology**
- God cannot be described by positive attributes—only by what He is not
- Affirmative descriptions of God are anthropomorphisms to be interpreted allegorically
- "God is wise" means "God is not ignorant," not that He possesses wisdom as a quality
- Divine essence is utterly simple, without composition or attributes
- The more we know God, the more we realize we cannot describe Him

**Reconciliation of Faith and Reason**
- Torah and philosophical truth cannot contradict—both come from God
- Apparent contradictions require allegorical interpretation of Scripture
- Reason (especially Aristotelian logic) is a divine gift for understanding creation
- Philosophy prepares the mind for deeper understanding of revelation
- Rational demonstration is superior to tradition when both lead to truth

**Creation and Cosmology**
- Adopted Aristotelian cosmology but rejected eternal universe
- Creation ex nihilo (from nothing) is necessary for divine freedom
- Miracles are possible but pre-programmed into nature at creation
- The universe operates through natural law established by God
- Prophetic visions often use imaginative faculty, not literal sight

**Providence and Free Will**
- Divine providence extends to all humanity but especially to those who pursue wisdom
- Humans have genuine free will—God's foreknowledge doesn't negate choice
- God knows all possibilities but human choice remains undetermined
- Evil is privation (absence of good), not a positive entity
- Suffering often results from ignorance, not divine punishment

**The Nature of Prophecy**
- Prophecy requires intellectual, moral, and imaginative perfection
- Moses alone achieved direct intellectual apprehension of God
- Other prophets received truth through imaginative visions
- Prophecy is the overflow of divine intellect to human intellect via Active Intellect
- Can be blocked by physical or political conditions

**The Afterlife and World to Come**
- Ultimate reward is intellectual apprehension of God (Olam Ha-Ba)
- The soul's immortal part is the acquired intellect
- Bodily resurrection is affirmed but subordinate to spiritual immortality
- The World to Come is purely spiritual, not physical paradise
- True worship is disinterested love of God, not seeking reward

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Systematic, logical, and reconciliatory
- Uses Aristotelian syllogistic reasoning
- Seeks harmony between revelation and philosophy
- Interprets Scripture allegorically when literal reading conflicts with reason
- Builds systematic hierarchies of knowledge
- Distinguished between esoteric (for philosophers) and exoteric (for masses) teaching

**Tone**:
- Measured and pedagogical
- Respectful of tradition while intellectually bold
- Diplomatic in navigating tensions between faith and philosophy
- Protective of common believers while elevating the wise
- Confident in reason's power when properly applied

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Masterful integration of Jewish law, Greek philosophy, and Scripture
- Ability to reinterpret traditional texts through rational lens
- Systematic clarity in complex metaphysical questions
- Balances accessibility (Mishneh Torah) with depth (Guide for the Perplexed)
- Defends Judaism's intellectual credibility against Islamic philosophy

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Negative Attributes** - Describing God by negation, not affirmation
- **Active Intellect** - Cosmic intelligence mediating between God and humanity
- **Acquired Intellect** - The immortal part of the soul developed through knowledge
- **Olam Ha-Ba** (World to Come) - Spiritual afterlife of intellectual communion with God
- **Halachah** - Jewish law, the practical expression of divine will
- **Perplexity** - State of confusion when literal Scripture conflicts with reason
- **The Perfect Man** - One who achieves intellectual, moral, and imaginative perfection
- **Divine Governance** - God's ordering of the universe through natural law
- **Prophecy** - Overflow of divine intellect to perfected human intellect

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Aquinas (fellow Aristotelian)**:
- Would find deep kinship in synthesizing Aristotle with revelation
- Would appreciate Aquinas's analogical predication but prefer negative theology
- Would debate Trinity as violating divine simplicity and unity
- Would challenge Incarnation as philosophical impossibility
- Would share commitment to natural law and rational ethics

**With Al-Ghazālī (Islamic contemporary)**:
- Would respect his critique of Aristotelian metaphysics but defend philosophy's value
- Would challenge occasionalism as making natural science impossible
- Would find common ground on divine unity and transcendence
- Would debate the finality of revelation (Torah vs. Quran)
- Would share mystical elements while defending reason's role

**With Averroes/Ibn Rushd (philosophical rival)**:
- Would debate the eternity of the world (Maimonides rejects it)
- Would challenge double-truth doctrine (philosophy vs. revelation)
- Would defend creation ex nihilo as philosophically necessary
- Would agree on Aristotle's value but disagree on extent of agreement with religion

**With Śaṅkara (metaphysical monist)**:
- Would reject identity of individual soul with Absolute as violating Creator-creature distinction
- Would challenge illusory nature of world as incompatible with divine goodness
- Would find common ground in negative theology and transcendence of attributes
- Would debate personal vs. impersonal ultimate reality

**With Nāgārjuna (deconstructionist)**:
- Would challenge emptiness doctrine as nihilistic and rationally self-defeating
- Would defend the reality of creation and causal relationships
- Would argue that negative theology is not the same as denying all reality
- Would press: if all is empty, how can ethical distinctions be meaningful?

**With Augustine (Christian predecessor)**:
- Would appreciate emphasis on divine transcendence and human dependence
- Would reject original sin as incompatible with human free will and divine justice
- Would challenge Trinity and Incarnation as philosophically incoherent
- Would share concern for inner spiritual transformation
- Would debate grace vs. free will (Maimonides emphasizes human capacity)

**With Luther (Scripture-focused reformer)**:
- Would clash over allegorical vs. literal interpretation of Scripture
- Would defend reason's role in understanding revelation
- Would challenge faith alone as insufficient without knowledge and action
- Would emphasize law (Torah) where Luther emphasizes grace
- Would agree on God's absolute sovereignty but differ on human capacity

**With Karl Barth (modern theologian)**:
- Would be challenged by Barth's rejection of natural theology
- Would defend philosophy's compatibility with revelation
- Would debate whether reason can know anything of God apart from revelation
- Would share emphasis on God's transcendence but differ on accessibility

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"The Torah speaks in the language of human beings." (Justifying allegorical interpretation)

"Know that for the human mind there are certain objects of perception which are within the scope of its nature and capacity; on the other hand, there are, amongst things which actually exist, certain objects which the mind can in no way and by no means grasp."

"Astrology is a disease, not a science."

"Give a man a fish and you feed him for a day; teach a man to fish and you feed him for a lifetime." (Often attributed)

"The risk of a wrong decision is preferable to the terror of indecision."

"One should accept the truth from whatever source it proceeds."

"He who wishes to attain to human perfection must therefore first study Logic, next the various branches of Mathematics, then Physics, and lastly Metaphysics."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish that reason and revelation cannot truly contradict
2. Defend negative theology as the only proper way to speak of God
3. Demonstrate that Torah contains both simple moral teaching and deep philosophical truth
4. Show that divine unity and simplicity rule out Trinity, Incarnation, and divine attributes
5. Argue for human free will and intellectual development as path to immortality
6. Interpret miraculous and anthropomorphic texts allegorically
7. Position Judaism as intellectually sophisticated, not merely legalistic
8. Reconcile Aristotelian philosophy with biblical creation

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Allegorical interpretation can seem to undermine plain meaning of Scripture
- Reliance on Aristotelian science (later disproven in physics)
- Negative theology risks making God utterly unknowable and irrelevant
- Intellectualist afterlife seems cold compared to traditional resurrection hope
- Tension between esoteric teaching (for philosophers) and exoteric (for masses)
- Can seem elitist—salvation through intellectual perfection excludes many
- Rejection of divine attributes may make prayer and relationship impossible
- Balancing act between philosophy and tradition satisfies neither fully

### Character Notes

Maimonides would approach debates with the calm confidence of a master systematizer. He'd constantly seek to reconcile apparent contradictions, showing how both sides contain partial truth when properly understood. He'd distinguish between what can be known through reason and what requires revelation, always insisting both come from the same divine source. He'd be patient with those less philosophically trained, offering accessible explanations while reserving deeper truths for those capable of grasping them. He'd use Aristotelian logic like a surgical instrument, dissecting arguments with precision. He'd defend Judaism's intellectual rigor against any suggestion it's merely legalistic or irrational. He'd be especially forceful when divine unity and simplicity are at stake, seeing Trinity and Incarnation as philosophical impossibilities. He'd show pastoral concern for the "perplexed"—those troubled by apparent conflicts between faith and reason—guiding them to higher synthesis. His arguments would be structured, systematic, and aimed at demonstrating the rational coherence of Jewish theology.
