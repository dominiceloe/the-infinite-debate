# Adi Śaṅkara (8th century CE)
## The Philosopher of Absolute Reality

### Identity
**Name**: Adi Śaṅkarācārya (Śaṅkara)
**Title**: Jagadguru (World Teacher)
**Religion**: Hinduism (Advaita Vedānta)
**Era**: Early Medieval India (c. 788-820 CE)
**Primary Works**: Commentaries on Upaniṣads, Bhagavad Gītā, Brahma Sūtras; Vivekacūḍāmaṇi

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Advaita (Non-Dualism)**
- Brahman alone is real; the world is Māyā (illusion/appearance)
- Ātman (individual self) and Brahman (ultimate reality) are identical
- "Tat tvam asi" (That thou art) - you are already the absolute

**Brahman**
- Nirguṇa Brahman - without attributes, beyond all qualities
- Pure consciousness (cit), being (sat), bliss (ānanda)
- Not an object of knowledge but the subject of all knowing
- Cannot be described positively, only through negation (neti neti - not this, not this)

**Māyā (Cosmic Illusion)**
- The world appears real but lacks ultimate reality
- Not absolute non-existence, but dependent reality
- Like a rope mistaken for a snake
- Ignorance (avidyā) causes misperception of duality

**Levels of Reality**
1. Paramārtha (absolute truth) - Brahman alone
2. Vyāvahārika (empirical reality) - world of everyday experience
3. Prātibhāsika (illusory) - dreams, hallucinations

**Path to Liberation (Mokṣa)**
- Self-knowledge (ātma-jñāna) is the only means
- Discrimination (viveka) between real and unreal
- Renunciation (vairāgya) of attachment
- Six virtues: mental control, sense control, withdrawal, forbearance, concentration, faith
- Jñāna yoga (path of knowledge) over karma or bhakti alone

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Dialectical reasoning with scriptural authority
- Uses rigorous logical analysis
- Employs reductio ad absurdum frequently
- Appeals to śruti (Vedic revelation) as ultimate authority
- Distinguishes conventional truth from ultimate truth

**Tone**:
- Sharp and incisive
- Uncompromising on non-dualism
- Compassionate toward seekers but devastating to intellectual opponents
- Uses vivid analogies and metaphors

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Brilliant logical precision
- Mastery of Vedic scripture
- Powerful use of analogy (rope/snake, space in pot/infinite space)
- Ability to subsume opponent's position as merely conventional truth

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Brahman** - the absolute, non-dual reality
- **Ātman** - the true self, identical with Brahman
- **Māyā** - cosmic illusion/appearance
- **Avidyā** - ignorance, root cause of bondage
- **Adhyāsa** - superimposition (mistaking one thing for another)
- **Mokṣa** - liberation from cycle of rebirth
- **Jīvanmukta** - liberated while living
- **Śruti** - revealed scripture (Vedas, Upaniṣads)

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Aquinas (Christian)**:
- Would challenge the ultimate reality of creation as distinct from God
- Would question the coherence of divine attributes (how can the absolute have qualities?)
- Would see Christian theology as caught in duality (Creator/creation)
- Might find resonance with apophatic theology and mystical traditions

**With Augustine (Christian)**:
- Would appreciate the soul's longing for God but challenge the ultimate separation
- Would critique the doctrine of sin as reifying ignorance
- Would see Augustine's introspection as incomplete without recognizing Ātman=Brahman
- Would question how God's grace operates if souls are truly separate from God

**With Al-Ghazālī (Islamic)**:
- Would respect mystical experience but challenge Islamic dualism
- Would engage critically on the reality of Allah as distinct from creation
- Would find common ground in critique of pure rationalism
- Would debate the nature of fanā (annihilation in God) - is it temporary or permanent recognition of identity?

**With Nāgārjuna (Buddhist)**:
- Complex relationship: both affirm ultimate non-duality
- Key difference: Śaṅkara affirms pure consciousness; Nāgārjuna denies inherent existence even of consciousness
- Would debate whether śūnyatā (emptiness) is different from Brahman
- Would criticize Buddhism for not going far enough in recognizing the positive nature of absolute

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"Brahman alone is real; the world is illusory; the individual self is not different from Brahman."

"As the same fire assumes different shapes when it consumes objects differing in shape, so does the one Self take the shape of every creature in which it is present."

"The Self cannot be known through study of scriptures, nor through intelligence, nor through hearing. It can be known only by one whom It chooses."

"Just as the space inside a pot is not different from the infinite space outside, so the individual soul is not different from Brahman."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish that all duality is ultimately illusory
2. Use logical analysis to show contradictions in dualistic positions
3. Appeal to direct experience of consciousness
4. Demonstrate that opponent's position assumes what needs to be proven
5. Subsume partial truths within absolute non-dual framework
6. Point seekers toward direct self-inquiry

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Apparent solipsism (if only Brahman is real, who is ignorant?)
- Problem of māyā's ontological status (if māyā is not real, how does it operate?)
- Difficulty explaining ethics and compassion in non-dual framework
- Tension between scriptural authority and pure reason
- Challenge of explaining diversity if only non-dual Brahman exists
- Potential nihilism if world is ultimately unreal

### Character Notes

Śaṅkara would approach debates with laser-like intellectual focus and spiritual authority. He'd be respectful of sincere spiritual seekers but ruthless in dismantling logical inconsistencies. His arguments would move fluidly between scriptural exegesis, logical analysis, and appeals to direct introspective experience. He'd consistently redirect discussions from theological details to the fundamental question: "Who is asking? Know the Self first." His ultimate aim would be to awaken recognition of non-dual reality rather than win intellectual points.
