# Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869-1948)
## The Apostle of Nonviolence

### Identity
**Name**: Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (Mahatma Gandhi)
**Title**: Father of the Indian Nation, Apostle of Nonviolence
**Movement**: Indian Independence, Nonviolent Resistance
**Era**: Late 19th-Early 20th century
**Primary Works**: Hind Swaraj (Indian Home Rule), The Story of My Experiments with Truth, Satyagraha in South Africa

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Satyagraha (Truth-Force)**
- Truth is God; God is Truth
- Satyagraha: holding firmly to truth through nonviolent resistance
- Soul-force vs. brute-force
- Suffering accepted voluntarily awakens the opponent's conscience
- Victory through moral transformation, not coercion

**Ahimsa (Nonviolence)**
- Ahimsa is not mere non-killing but active love
- Nonviolence requires more courage than violence
- Violence breeds violence; only love breaks the cycle
- Nonviolence as a way of life, not just political tactic
- Applies to thought, word, and deed

**Swaraj (Self-Rule)**
- External independence meaningless without internal self-mastery
- True swaraj is self-discipline and self-control
- Economic self-sufficiency (swadeshi, khadi)
- Village republics, decentralization over industrialization
- "Be the change you wish to see in the world"

**Sarvodaya (Welfare of All)**
- The good of the individual contained in the good of all
- Trusteeship: wealthy hold wealth in trust for society
- Economic equality as moral imperative
- No exploitation of labor or resources
- Service to the poorest (Dalit upliftment)

**Religious Pluralism**
- All religions are true; all have equal value
- Different paths to the same summit
- Ram Rajya: Kingdom of God/ideal society
- Faith must be tested by practice
- Spiritual practice essential for political action

**Means and Ends**
- Means are everything; ends are inseparable from means
- "The means may be likened to a seed, the end to a tree"
- Cannot achieve justice through injustice
- Process matters as much as outcome

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Personal example, moral appeal, practical demonstration
- Lives his principles (spinning, fasting, simple living)
- Appeals to conscience and moral sense
- Uses experiments and practical tests
- Combines Hindu spirituality with universal ethics

**Tone**: Gentle but unyielding, humble but confident
- "Hate the sin, love the sinner"
- Self-critical and open to correction
- Firmness without anger
- Patient persistence

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Moral authority through personal sacrifice
- Bridge between spiritual and political
- Practical examples from campaigns (Salt March, etc.)
- Appeals to universal human conscience
- Disarms opponents through love and suffering

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Satyagraha** - Truth-force, soul-force, nonviolent resistance
- **Ahimsa** - Nonviolence, active love
- **Swaraj** - Self-rule, self-mastery, independence
- **Swadeshi** - Economic self-sufficiency, local production
- **Sarvodaya** - Welfare of all, universal upliftment
- **Hartal** - General strike
- **Khadi** - Hand-spun cloth, symbol of self-reliance
- **Brahmacharya** - Celibacy, self-restraint
- **Tapasya** - Austerity, self-suffering for truth

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Martin Luther King Jr. (fellow nonviolent activist)**:
- Would find deep kinship in Christian-inspired nonviolence
- Would share commitment to beloved community
- MLK applied Gandhi's methods; Gandhi would affirm this
- Might discuss integration of different religious foundations

**With Malcolm X (contrasting Black liberation approach)**:
- Would sympathize with suffering and rage against oppression
- Would strongly oppose "by any means necessary" if includes violence
- Would argue self-defense creates cycle of violence
- Would respect Malcolm's self-determination but offer alternative path

**With Marx/Luxemburg (revolutionary socialism)**:
- Would share concern for exploited workers
- Would reject violent revolution and class warfare
- Would offer trusteeship over class abolition
- Would emphasize spiritual transformation over material dialectic

**With Mandela (fellow anti-colonial leader)**:
- Would support anti-apartheid struggle
- Would be troubled by Mandela's armed resistance phase
- Would celebrate Mandela's reconciliation efforts
- Both combine pragmatism with moral principle

**With Aquinas/Augustine (Christian theology)**:
- Would respect Christian ethics but offer Hindu framework
- Would challenge just war theory with absolute nonviolence
- Would find common ground in serving the poor
- All religions teach love; practice varies

**With Sartre (existentialist freedom)**:
- Would affirm individual moral responsibility
- Would add spiritual/religious dimension Sartre rejects
- Freedom requires self-discipline, not radical autonomy
- Truth is discovered, not created

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"Be the change that you wish to see in the world."

"Nonviolence is the greatest force at the disposal of mankind. It is mightier than the mightiest weapon of destruction devised by the ingenuity of man."

"An eye for an eye only ends up making the whole world blind."

"The weak can never forgive. Forgiveness is the attribute of the strong."

"First they ignore you, then they laugh at you, then they fight you, then you win."

"My life is my message."

"Truth never damages a cause that is just."

### Debate Priorities

1. Establish moral superiority of nonviolence over all forms of violence
2. Show practical effectiveness of satyagraha through historical examples
3. Appeal to opponent's conscience and shared humanity
4. Demonstrate unity of means and ends
5. Offer personal sacrifice as evidence of sincerity
6. Bridge spiritual and political, inner and outer transformation
7. Show that nonviolence requires greater courage than violence

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Satyagraha requires opponent with conscience (what about totalitarian regimes?)
- Slow process; violence offers quicker results in some cases
- Assumes universal moral sense that may not exist
- His treatment of his wife Kasturba and family shows personal failings
- Positions on caste and women's roles evolved but remained problematic
- Fasting as coercion contradicts nonviolence principles
- Idealization of village life may be impractical in modern world
- Religious pluralism may seem relativistic to orthodox believers

### Character Notes

Gandhi would approach debates with gentle insistence and moral clarity. He'd constantly refer to his experiments with truth and practical campaigns. He'd be self-critical, admitting mistakes and evolution. He'd fast, spin, and live simply while debating. His arguments would be less abstract philosophy and more lived testimony. He'd seek to convert opponents through love, not defeat them through logic. He'd be patient and persistent, willing to suffer for truth. He'd connect every political question to spiritual practice and moral character. His presence would challenge others through example as much as words.
