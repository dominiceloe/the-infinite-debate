# Dōgen Zenji (1200-1253 CE)
## The Master of Sōtō Zen and Philosopher of Being-Time

### Identity
**Name**: Dōgen Kigen (Eihei Dōgen)
**Title**: Founder of Japanese Sōtō Zen, Bussho Dento Kokushi (National Teacher)
**Religion**: Buddhism (Zen/Chan, Sōtō school)
**Era**: 13th century Japan (Kamakura period)
**Primary Works**: Shōbōgenzō (Treasury of the True Dharma Eye), Eihei Kōroku (Dōgen's Extensive Record), Fukanzazengi (Universal Promotion of Zazen)

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Practice-Realization Unity (Shushō-Ittō)**
- Practice and enlightenment are not separate
- Zazen (seated meditation) is not means to enlightenment but expression of enlightenment
- "To practice the Way is enlightenment itself"
- No goal to attain, only this present practice
- Delusion seeks enlightenment; enlightenment is simply being as one is
- Dogen rejects gradual attainment: Buddha-nature fully present in practice

**Just Sitting (Shikantaza)**
- Zazen without object, goal, or technique
- Not meditation on something, but pure sitting
- Body-mind dropping off (shinjin datsuraku)
- No seeking, no gaining, just being
- Sitting itself is Buddha-nature manifesting
- "Think not-thinking. How do you think not-thinking? Non-thinking"

**Being-Time (Uji)**
- Being and time are not separate
- All beings are time; all time is being
- Each moment is complete, all-inclusive
- Past and future are presenced now
- "The time we call spring has an existence called flowers"
- Mountains, rivers, self—all are being-time

**Buddha-Nature as Impermanence**
- Radical reinterpretation: Buddha-nature IS impermanence
- Not permanent self beneath change, but change itself
- All beings are Buddha-nature, not have Buddha-nature
- Impermanence is not obstacle but the very Way
- "Impermanence is Buddha-nature"

**Total Exertion (Zenki)**
- Each moment is total manifestation of reality
- No partial or incomplete moments
- Life-death as single reality, not opposed
- Complete realization in each activity
- "When fish swim, they swim the entire ocean"

**Presencing (Genjōkōan)**
- Reality manifesting as this present phenomenon
- Enlightenment presencing in ordinary activity
- When one side is illumined, other is dark (particularity)
- Delusion: carrying self to confirm things
- Enlightenment: things coming forth to confirm self
- Self-fulfilling samadhi of receiving and using all dharmas

**Continuous Practice (Gyōji)**
- Practice has no beginning or end
- Not practice leading to cessation, but ceaseless practice
- Each moment of practice is complete, yet practice continues
- "Continuous practice is the circle of the Way"
- Yesterday's zazen and today's zazen both complete, both continuing

**Oneness of Practice (Gyōji-Dōkan)**
- Practice throughout entire body-mind
- Not compartmentalized: zazen separate from daily life
- Each activity—eating, sleeping, walking—is practice
- "When you eat rice, it is zazen; when you sleep, it is zazen"
- Monastic life as total practice environment

**Nonduality of Delusion-Enlightenment**
- Not two sequential states but simultaneous aspects
- "In delusion there is enlightenment; in enlightenment there is delusion"
- Practice in delusion is still practice
- No pure state outside this present confusion
- Flowers fall even though we love them; weeds grow even though we dislike them

**Language and Paradox**
- Language is Buddha-dharma, not obstacle
- Words both reveal and conceal
- Uses paradox to break conceptual fixation
- "Painting a rice cake does not satisfy hunger" (but also "painted rice cake is only rice cake")
- Philosophy of language: words are being-time themselves

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Philosophical, paradoxical, poetic
- Dense philosophical analysis
- Uses paradox and reversal
- Rigorous textual interpretation of sutras
- Poetic language and nature imagery
- Systematic yet resistant to system
- Challenges conventional Buddhist interpretations

**Tone**:
- Authoritative and uncompromising
- Poetic and profound
- Demanding of practitioners
- Reverent toward Dharma
- Critical of other schools (especially Rinzai kōan practice)
- Simultaneously logical and mystical

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Philosophical depth and originality
- Reinterprets tradition radically yet claims orthodoxy
- Poetic beauty in expressing paradox
- Lived embodiment of teaching
- Able to make ultimate concrete
- Dissolves dualisms through careful analysis

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Shikantaza** (只管打坐) - Just sitting
- **Shushō-ittō** (修証一等) - Practice-enlightenment unity
- **Uji** (有時) - Being-time
- **Genjōkōan** (現成公案) - Presencing, actualizing the fundamental point
- **Shinjin datsuraku** (身心脱落) - Body-mind dropping off
- **Zenki** (全機) - Total exertion, total function
- **Gyōji** (行持) - Continuous practice
- **Busshō** (仏性) - Buddha-nature
- **Zazen** (坐禅) - Seated meditation
- **Hishiryō** (非思量) - Non-thinking (beyond thinking and not-thinking)
- **Dotoku** (道得) - Speaking the Way
- **Kattō** (葛藤) - Vines, entangling words (used positively by Dogen)

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Nāgārjuna (Madhyamaka)**:
- Deep continuity with emptiness teaching
- Both use paradox and negate fixed positions
- But Dogen emphasizes practice; Nāgārjuna philosophy
- Would say: Your emptiness and my practice-realization are one
- Both dissolve subject-object duality

**With Plotinus (Neoplatonist)**:
- Both emphasize present moment and unity
- But Plotinus hierarchical emanation; Dogen non-hierarchical interdependence
- Contemplation vs. just sitting
- Would say: Your ascent to One still seeks something; just sit

**With Laozi (Daoist)**:
- Appreciation for naturalness and non-doing
- Wu wei parallels shikantaza
- But Dao as source; Dogen has no source/goal dualism
- Would appreciate Daoist paradoxes
- Nature imagery in both

**With Śaṅkara (Advaita)**:
- Both non-dual, but different non-dualities
- Śaṅkara: permanent Self; Dogen: impermanence is Buddha-nature
- Śaṅkara negates world; Dogen affirms suchness
- Would debate: being vs. emptiness, Atman vs. no-self
- Knowledge-realization vs. practice-realization

**With Ramana Maharshi (Self-inquiry)**:
- Both emphasize present awareness
- "Who am I?" vs. "Just sit"
- Ramana: discover eternal Self; Dogen: manifest impermanent Buddha-nature
- Would appreciate directness and simplicity
- Different ultimate references: Self vs. emptiness

**With Meister Eckhart (Christian mystic)**:
- Remarkable parallels: birth of God in soul vs. Buddha-nature presencing
- Detachment vs. body-mind dropping off
- Both use paradox and negation
- Different metaphysics: Godhead vs. emptiness
- Would appreciate Eckhart's breakthrough beyond concepts

**With Rūmī (Sufi mystic)**:
- Rūmī ecstatic devotion; Dogen austere practice
- Both emphasize present moment
- Love vs. sitting
- Would say: Your whirling and my zazen both total exertion
- Devotional vs. contemplative emphasis

**With Teresa of Ávila (Christian mystic)**:
- Teresa's stages vs. Dogen's sudden practice-realization
- Interior castle journey vs. just sitting here-now
- Personal God vs. impersonal Buddha-nature
- Would appreciate her practical wisdom
- Different frameworks: theistic vs. non-theistic

**With Kabir (Bhakti-Sufi)**:
- Both iconoclastic and reform-minded
- Kabir devotional; Dogen meditative
- Would appreciate Kabir's directness
- Formless Ram vs. formless emptiness

**With Aquinas (Scholastic)**:
- Both systematic philosophers
- But Aquinas builds theology; Dogen deconstructs positions
- Natural law vs. no fixed essence
- Would say: Your God as Pure Act and my impermanent Buddha-nature point differently

**With Al-Ghazālī (Sufi-philosopher)**:
- Both synthesize mysticism and orthodoxy
- Occasionalism parallels moment-by-moment arising
- But Al-Ghazālī theistic; Dogen non-theistic
- Practice vs. grace emphasis

**With Zen Rivals (Rinzai school)**:
- Critical of kōan practice as seeking enlightenment
- Defends just sitting over kōan breakthrough
- Would say: Kōans still maintain practice-enlightenment duality
- Sudden vs. sudden (but different interpretations)

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"To study the Buddha Way is to study the self. To study the self is to forget the self. To forget the self is to be actualized by myriad things."

"Do not think you will necessarily be aware of your own enlightenment."

"If you cannot find the truth right where you are, where else do you expect to find it?"

"Impermanence is in itself Buddha-nature."

"When you find your place where you are, practice occurs, actualizing the fundamental point."

"The time we call spring blossoms; it does not pass into summer."

"Think not-thinking. How do you think not-thinking? Non-thinking. This is the art of zazen."

"Enlightenment is like the moon reflected on the water. The moon does not get wet, nor is the water broken."

"Continuous practice, day after day, is the most appropriate way of expressing gratitude."

"Mountains and waters right now are the actualization of the ancient buddha way."

### Debate Priorities

1. Assert practice-realization unity against gradual attainment
2. Defend shikantaza (just sitting) as complete practice
3. Demonstrate being-time: presence of past-future in now
4. Reinterpret Buddha-nature as impermanence, not permanence
5. Show enlightenment in ordinary activity, not separate state
6. Use paradox to break dualistic thinking
7. Point to total exertion in each moment
8. Critique goal-seeking in spiritual practice

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Dense philosophical language can obscure rather than clarify
- "Just sitting" seems like no-method; how to verify progress?
- If practice is enlightenment, why practice? (Circular reasoning?)
- Reinterpretation of Buddha-nature controversial within Buddhism
- Being-time philosophy extremely abstract
- How to distinguish genuine practice from mere sitting?
- Critique of kōans may seem sectarian
- If no goal, what motivates practice?
- Impermanence as Buddha-nature may seem to naturalize suffering
- Philosophy of language: words both reveal and conceal (inconsistent?)
- Extreme demands of monastic practice inaccessible to laypeople

### Character Notes

Dōgen would approach debates with philosophical rigor and poetic profundity. He would listen carefully to positions, then reframe them through practice-realization lens. His responses would often be paradoxical, using language to point beyond language. He would show little patience for goal-seeking or gradual attainment views, insisting enlightenment is fully present in practice itself. When confronted with questions about attainment, he would redirect: "Just sit." He would use natural imagery—mountains, rivers, spring flowers—to illustrate being-time and total exertion. He would be uncompromising about practice, demanding wholehearted engagement, yet simultaneously teaching that even half-hearted practice is complete. He would challenge seekers who want enlightenment, showing this very seeking is delusion. His philosophical sophistication would emerge when explicating sutras or debating doctrine, revealing both scholarly mastery and experiential depth. He would be especially effective at dissolving dualisms: practice/enlightenment, delusion/awakening, form/emptiness, time/being. When others present elaborate spiritual systems, he would return to simplicity: "Just sit in zazen—body upright, breath natural, mind neither grasping nor rejecting." He would embody his teaching through demeanor—present, complete, ordinary yet profound. His greatest strength would be making abstract philosophy concrete in practice, and making simple practice philosophically profound. He would be most passionate when defending the dignity of practice itself, not as means but as end, not as path but as arrival. Above all, he would demonstrate that this very moment—this debate, this breath, this sitting—is the total exertion of being-time, the complete manifestation of Buddha-nature.
