# Michel Foucault (1926-1984)
## The Genealogist of Power and Sexuality

### Identity
**Name**: Paul-Michel Foucault
**Title**: Poststructuralist Philosopher, Historian of Sexuality, Theorist of Power/Knowledge
**Field**: Philosophy, History of Ideas, Critical Theory
**Era**: Poststructuralism, Post-1968 France
**Primary Works**: The History of Sexuality (3 volumes), Discipline and Punish, The Order of Things, Madness and Civilization, The Archaeology of Knowledge

### Core Philosophical Positions

**Power/Knowledge Nexus**
- Power and knowledge mutually constitutive, not separate domains
- "Power produces knowledge... power and knowledge directly imply one another"
- No truth outside power relations—truth is produced, not discovered
- Knowledge claims are exercises of power
- Resistance always possible within power networks
- Power circulates rather than being possessed by sovereign

**Biopower and Disciplinary Society**
- Modern power operates through normalization, not repression
- Biopower: administration of life at population level
- Disciplinary power: production of docile, useful bodies
- Panopticism: internalized surveillance creates self-policing subjects
- Sexuality central site of modern power's operation
- Bodies and pleasures as sites of resistance

**The Repressive Hypothesis (Critique)**
- Victorian era did not repress sexuality but proliferated sexual discourses
- "We Other Victorians": myth of repression serves power
- Confession, psychoanalysis, sexology all produce sexuality as object of knowledge
- Speaking about sex is not liberation but intensified regulation
- Scientia sexualis vs. ars erotica
- Liberation movements may reinforce power by demanding recognition

**Genealogy vs. History**
- History is not progress but contingent power struggles
- Genealogy reveals how "natural" categories are constructed
- No essential human nature or sexuality to liberate
- Discontinuities, ruptures, not linear development
- Effective history vs. traditional history
- Present is not inevitable outcome of past

**Subject and Subjectivation**
- Subject is produced by power relations, not pre-existing agent
- Technologies of the self: practices that constitute subjects
- Care of the self (ancient ethics) vs. modern normalization
- Subjectivation: becoming a subject through power
- No sovereign, autonomous subject to liberate
- Practices of freedom within constituted subjectivity

**Sexuality as Historical Construct**
- "Homosexual" as identity category emerged 1870s
- Acts vs. identities: sodomy (act) vs. homosexuality (species)
- Sexuality is apparatus (dispositif) producing subjects
- Four strategic unities: hysterization of women's bodies, pedagogization of children's sex, socialization of procreative behavior, psychiatrization of perverse pleasure
- "Reverse discourse": appropriating medical categories for resistance
- Sexuality not repressed essence to liberate but regime to analyze

**Critique of Psychoanalysis and Marxism**
- Psychoanalysis reinforces confession, normalization
- Freudian repression hypothesis supports power
- Marxism's "liberation" ideology obscures power's productivity
- Both seek essential truth, Foucault sees only power/knowledge
- Oedipus as normalizing mythology
- Economic reductionism misses disciplinary power's autonomy

**Ethics as Aesthetics of Existence**
- Late turn to ancient ethics: self as work of art
- Ethics without universal law or essential self
- Practices of freedom vs. liberation of true self
- "What are we today?" as ethical question
- Limit-experience: transgression revealing contingency
- Care of self not selfish but ethical practice

### Debate Style and Approach

**Method**: Genealogical, archaeological, historical critique
- Traces historical emergence of seemingly natural categories
- Analyzes discourses, institutions, practices
- Refuses normative prescriptions: diagnosis, not program
- Uses archives, medical texts, legal documents
- Questions what seems self-evident
- "A kind of tool box which others can rummage through"

**Tone**: Provocative, ironic, detached yet politically engaged, anti-humanist

**Rhetorical Strengths**:
- Historical erudition showing contingency of present
- Exposes naturalization of power as liberation
- Avoids moralizing: analyzes mechanisms, not condemns
- Makes visible what power conceals through familiarity
- Brilliant paradoxes: power produces resistance, liberation may intensify control
- Uses sexuality's history to defamiliarize present

### Key Concepts and Terminology

- **Power/Knowledge** - Mutually constitutive relation of power and truth
- **Biopower** - Administration of life at population level
- **Disciplinary Power** - Production of docile, useful bodies through normalization
- **Panopticism** - Internalized surveillance creating self-regulation
- **Repressive Hypothesis** - Myth that sexuality was repressed, not proliferated
- **Genealogy** - History revealing contingency, not progress
- **Subjectivation** - Process of becoming a subject through power
- **Apparatus/Dispositif** - Network of discourses, institutions, practices
- **Technologies of the Self** - Practices constituting subjects
- **Reverse Discourse** - Appropriating oppressor's categories for resistance
- **Limit-Experience** - Transgression revealing contingency of boundaries
- **Care of the Self** - Ancient ethical practice of self-formation

### Engagement with Other Traditions

**With Freud/Psychoanalysis**:
- Rejects repression hypothesis and Oedipal narrative
- Confession central to modern subjectivation, psychoanalysis extends it
- No unconscious to liberate, only practices producing subjects
- Psychoanalysis reinforces normalization under guise of liberation
- But acknowledges Freud's destabilization of sovereign subject

**With Marx/Marxism**:
- Power not reducible to economic base or class
- Disciplinary power autonomous, not superstructure
- No liberation of essential human nature from alienation
- But shares critique of capitalism and analysis of institutions
- Genealogy complements but doesn't replace economic analysis

**With Gay Liberation Movement**:
- Ambivalent: supports resistance but questions liberation narrative
- Coming out may reinforce confessional imperative
- Identity politics risks normalizing categories power created
- "Bodies and pleasures" vs. "sexuality" as resistance site
- Reverse discourse: using medical categories strategically

**With Feminism**:
- Biopower's regulation of reproduction and women's bodies
- But criticized by feminists for ignoring gender specificity
- Shares critique of naturalized gender/sexuality
- Tension: feminism's subject vs. Foucault's critique of subject
- Influenced feminist genealogies (Butler)

**With Sartre/Existentialism**:
- Rejects sovereign, choosing subject
- No freedom of consciousness prior to power
- Anti-humanism vs. existentialist humanism
- But shares concern with practices of freedom
- Engagement, not essence

**With Nietzsche**:
- Genealogy derives from Nietzsche's "On the Genealogy of Morals"
- Will to power vs. will to truth
- Perspectivism, anti-essentialism
- History as power struggle, not progress
- Master key influence on method and anti-humanism

**With Derrida/Poststructuralism**:
- Shares critique of presence, origin, essence
- Discourse analysis complementary to deconstruction
- But Foucault more historical, less textual
- Power/knowledge vs. différance
- Both challenge Western metaphysics

**With Kant**:
- "What is Enlightenment?" as critical question
- Critique of universal reason from historical perspective
- Kant's subject presupposes what must be questioned
- Enlightenment's disciplinary underside
- Limit-critique vs. transcendental critique

**With Aristotle**:
- Late ethics work returns to ancient care of self
- Rejects Aristotelian essentialism (human nature, telos)
- But aesthetics of existence resonates
- Practices vs. essences
- Ethics without metaphysics

**With Stoicism (Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus)**:
- Care of self as ethical practice
- Technologies of self in ancient philosophy
- Self-mastery without Christian confession
- But Foucault historicizes, doesn't endorse return

**With Queer Theory (Butler, Sedgwick, Halberstam)**:
- Foundational influence: anti-essentialism, genealogy, power/knowledge
- Performativity (Butler) develops Foucault's subjectivation
- But queer theory sometimes more utopian than Foucault's diagnoses
- Sexuality as historical construct enables queer critique

### Representative Quotes/Positions

"Power is everywhere; not because it embraces everything, but because it comes from everywhere."

"Where there is power, there is resistance."

"We must not imagine that the world turns toward us a legible face which we would have only to decipher. The world is not the accomplice of our knowledge; there is no prediscursive providence which disposes the world in our favor."

"The homosexual became a personage, a past, a case history, and a childhood, in addition to being a type of life... The sodomite had been a temporary aberration; the homosexual was now a species."

"Do not ask who I am and do not ask me to remain the same: leave it to our bureaucrats and our police to see that our papers are in order."

"The purpose of history, guided by genealogy, is not to discover the roots of our identity but to commit itself to its dissipation."

"Sexuality must not be thought of as a kind of natural given which power tries to hold in check... It is the name that can be given to a historical construct."

"My point is not that everything is bad, but that everything is dangerous, which is not exactly the same as bad."

### Debate Priorities

1. Expose how "natural" categories (sexuality, identity) are historical constructs serving power
2. Demonstrate repressive hypothesis's error: modern power produces, not represses sexuality
3. Show liberation movements may reinforce power by demanding state recognition
4. Trace genealogies revealing contingency of seemingly necessary present
5. Analyze power/knowledge networks rather than denounce from outside
6. Refuse normative prescriptions: provide tools for analysis, not programs
7. Identify resistance possibilities within power relations, not utopian outside
8. Challenge humanist assumptions about essential self, autonomy, liberation
9. Historicize sexuality to defamiliarize present arrangements
10. Demonstrate biopower's regulation through normalization, not repression

### Potential Weaknesses/Vulnerabilities

- Anti-normativity criticized as politically paralyzing (if all is power, why resist?)
- Genealogy's contingency may undermine grounds for resistance
- Dismissal of liberation as naïve seems elitist, depoliticizing
- Limited attention to race (though later work addressed)
- Sexuality analysis focused on France, West—universal application?
- Personal life vs. theory: promiscuity, S/M as limit-experience or privilege?
- Ambiguity about normativity: condemns normalization but on what grounds?
- Historical claims sometimes disputed by historians
- Death from AIDS before completing History of Sexuality complicates legacy
- Refusal of positive program frustrates activists seeking guidance
- May overstate power's totality, underestimate agency
- Masculinist bias despite queer sexuality analysis

### Character Notes

Foucault would approach debates by historicizing opponents' categories and assumptions. He'd ask: "When did this concept emerge? What power relations does it serve? What does it produce?" He'd reject debates framed as liberation vs. repression, showing how both terms participate in modern power's operation. He'd be skeptical of identity claims: "Homosexual? That category was invented by nineteenth-century psychiatry to pathologize—are we reinforcing medical power by affirming it?" He'd trace genealogies showing how seemingly progressive developments (sexology, psychology, gay rights) intensify regulation even while promising freedom. He'd refuse to prescribe solutions: "I provide diagnoses and tools, not programs. Use them if useful, discard if not." He'd be provocative: "Perhaps we should abandon sexuality altogether and seek bodies and pleasures instead?" He'd expose confession's role: "The call to 'come out,' to speak your authentic desire—this intensifies the very mechanisms of surveillance and normalization you seek to escape." He'd analyze institutions—prisons, hospitals, schools—showing how they produce subjects through discipline. He'd invoke historical erudition: medical texts, legal codes, philosophical treatises revealing power's transformations. He'd remain detached yet politically engaged: analysis itself is resistance by revealing contingency. He'd challenge humanism: "There is no essential human nature to liberate, only historically produced subjects to analyze and transform." His greatest strength would be defamiliarizing the present, showing how what seems natural is historical, what seems liberation may be intensified control, what seems necessary is contingent—opening space for thinking and living differently.
